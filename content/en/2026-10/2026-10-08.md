### 1. [Adult ADHD Patients Face Significantly Higher Dementia Risk](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261007/Adults-with-ADHD-face-higher-risk-of-developing-dementia-later-in-life.aspx)

ADHD used to be considered only affecting children's attention. Now, new research from University of Pittsburgh School of Medicine shows that adults with ADHD face significantly higher risk of developing dementia or mild cognitive impairment in later life compared to the general population. The study tracked a large number of adult ADHD patients and found their cognitive decline risk was markedly increased. This provides new clues for understanding the connection between neurodevelopmental disorders and neurodegenerative diseases, but it remains unclear whether ADHD treatment can reduce this risk, and the specific neural mechanisms have not been revealed.

### 2. [Exercise Slows Epigenetic Aging and Improves Cognition in Cancer Survivors](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/exercise-slows-epigenetic-aging-and-improves-cognitive-function-in-cancer-survivors/)

Cancer survivors after chemotherapy typically show accelerated epigenetic aging. This randomized controlled trial recruited 124 breast cancer survivors, divided into exercise and health control groups, for 12 months. Results showed that the exercise group's GrimAge2 and DNAmFitAge epigenetic clocks were significantly slower than the control group, with changes in epigenetic age significantly correlated with improvements in attention and cognitive function. Genome-wide analysis also found that exercise altered methylation patterns of genes related to brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) signaling pathways. Current conclusions apply only to breast cancer survivors and have not been validated in other cancer types or healthy populations, nor has the optimal exercise protocol been determined.

**Source Type**: Professional aging research website / **Evidence Stage**: Randomized controlled trial / **Credibility**: High

### 3. [Transplanted Hearts Shift Toward Recipients' Molecular Age](https://lifespan.io/transplanted-hearts-shift-toward-recipients-molecular-age/)

Can organ transplantation truly "renew" the body? A preprint study from Harvard Medical School offers a surprising answer. The research team transplanted hearts between mice of different ages and measured with three epigenetic clocks after 4-6 months. Results showed that young hearts transplanted into old mice acquired older methylation patterns, while old hearts transplanted into young mice became younger. Mitochondria-related gene expression also changed accordingly. In 11 human heart transplant biopsy samples, two clocks similarly showed significant recipient age effects on transplanted hearts. This suggests the systemic environment reshapes organ biological age, but the study did not cover long-term outcomes or the impact of irreversible structural damage.

**Source Type**: Professional longevity research website / **Evidence Stage**: Preprint (animal experiments + small human sample data) / **Credibility**: Medium

### 8. [Intermittent Fasting Reduces Weight but Extra Protein Failed to Prevent Muscle Loss](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261007/Does-extra-protein-protect-aging-muscle-during-fasting.aspx)

How can older adults preserve muscle during weight loss? This randomized trial recruited elderly men for alternate-day fasting, with one group receiving additional protein and leucine. Results showed that alternate-day fasting did reduce body weight, but the extra protein and leucine did not prevent lean mass (primarily muscle) loss. This suggests that simply increasing protein intake may be insufficient to protect aging muscle during caloric restriction and may need to be combined with resistance training or other interventions. The study involved only elderly men and a specific fasting protocol, not covering women or other age groups.

### 10. [Electrical Signals Sharpen Bioprocess Control](https://www.genengnews.com/topics/bioprocessing/electrical-signals-sharpen-bioprocess-control/)

Bioprocess engineers have always wanted to detect earlier when cell cultures start declining. A review from University of Massachusetts Amherst and Rochester Institute of Technology points out that cells' electrical properties can provide faster health windows. The research tracked 2015-2026 progress in using membrane capacitance, cytoplasmic conductivity, polarizability, and surface charge to assess cell state, without labeling or destruction. In Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells, dielectrophoresis (DEP) technology can track electrical signals of apoptosis: membrane remodeling causes membrane capacitance to decline, followed by cytoplasmic conductivity plummeting from about 0.45 S/m to 0.05 S/m. Commercial 3DEP platforms can now capture electrical characteristics from about 20,000 cells in seconds, detecting apoptotic subpopulations hours before traditional biochemical markers. A 2026 analysis combined electrical impedance data with supervised machine learning, with the tuned random forest model achieving 90% prediction accuracy, proposed as a "traffic light" system for batch monitoring. This provides a technical path for real-time automated control in biomanufacturing but has not yet become standard practice.

**Source Type**: Well-known biotech media / **Evidence Stage**: Review and technical validation / **Credibility**: Medium
