### 1. Granzyme K Released by Aged T Cells Accelerates Multi-Organ Aging

Aged CD8⁺ T cells (a type of immune cell that identifies and eliminates threats) pump out large amounts of a protease called Granzyme K, driving the aging of organs like the liver and heart, according to a [Lifespan.io report](https://lifespan.io/one-way-that-aged-t-cells-age-the-whole-body/). The research team compared mice lacking Granzyme K with normal mice. Older mice without this protease showed significantly reduced liver and heart fibrosis, suppressed ventricular wall thickening, and lower blood levels of proteins linked to cell damage and mitochondrial dysfunction. Surprisingly, Granzyme K had little effect on how the immune system itself ages, and no significant change in blood inflammation markers, suggesting it damages organs through a pathway that's independent of inflammation. The researchers also confirmed Granzyme K's presence in public datasets of human fibrotic diseases, but all functional evidence so far comes from mice. Whether the same effect translates to humans remains unclear.

### 2. Alzheimer's Genetic Risk Results Available Online for Self-Viewing, Psychological Impact Comparable to Doctor-Delivered Results

A randomized clinical trial of 651 adults found that viewing Alzheimer's genetic risk results through a structured digital platform was comparable to doctor-led video consultations in terms of knowledge gained, anxiety levels, and disease-specific psychological distress, according to a [News-Medical report](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261119/Your-Alzheimere28099s-test-results-are-ready-online-Would-you-open-them.aspx). Psychological outcomes after blood biomarker disclosure were similar between the two groups, but participants who received doctor consultations rated their satisfaction with the genetic results disclosure higher. This suggests digital disclosure has real potential for expanding test accessibility, but the satisfaction gap shows digital platforms can't fully replace human consultation yet.

### 3. Weekly Injection Cuts Liver Fat by 68% in 16 Weeks, Sample Size Only 19

DA-1726, a candidate drug from MetaVia, was given as a weekly injection to 15 obese but otherwise healthy adults in an early trial, and after 16 weeks liver fat dropped by an average of 68%, with abdominal fat and body weight also going down, according to a [Longevity.Technology report](https://longevity.technology/news/weekly-shot-cuts-liver-fat-68-in-small-early-trial/). The control group had just 4 people. Excess liver fat (clinically known as metabolic-associated fatty liver disease) is a major risk factor for cardiovascular and metabolic diseases. A sample of 19 people means this was only an early safety and preliminary signal exploration, and the results need validation in larger trials.

### 4. AI Scientist Framework DeepQuest Automatically Generates Verifiable Hypotheses in Alzheimer's Research

DeepQuest, an AI framework built for Alzheimer's disease research, takes unsolved problems from domain review papers and converts them into traceable, testable hypotheses, then uses data feasibility screening and grouped ranking to decide which ones to prioritize, according to a [paper index/abstract](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2611.26343). On the OASIS-3 dataset, the system generated 42 feasible hypotheses and outperformed baseline comparisons on 14 of 15 evaluation dimensions, with an audited scientific discovery rate of 95.24%. Findings related to neuropsychiatric symptoms and dementia risk, as well as the temporal stability of amyloid protein spatial distribution, were supported in independent cohorts. These are preprint-stage evaluation results, and whether the framework can actually speed up drug discovery still needs lab validation. Code is publicly available in an [anonymous repository](https://anonymous.4open.science/r/DeepQuest-3066/).

### 5. Improved Interpretability Method for Genotype-Embedded Models Aids Neurological Disease Prediction

The improved attribution method introduced in a [paper index/abstract](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2611.25681) explains how deep learning models that combine genetic data with brain imaging make neurological disease predictions, specifically tracking which genetic variants the model actually relies on for its decisions. Existing common methods (Integrated Gradients) have a technical flaw when handling genotypes: genotypes can only take three discrete values (0, 1, and 2), and linear interpolation paths pass through intermediate states that don't actually exist, making attribution results unreliable. The new method instead integrates along genotype paths that actually appear in the training data, achieving better attribution fidelity on a Parkinson's disease classification task. The value here is making it easier for clinicians to check a model's reasoning, but it's still a preprint and hasn't been peer-reviewed.

### 6. HIT-Fuse: A Multi-Omics and Pathology Image Fusion Framework for Multiple Cancer Prognosis Analyses

HIT-Fuse integrates six types of data, genomic copy number variation, DNA methylation, RNA sequencing, microRNA expression, protein abundance, and pathology slide image features, for cancer prognosis analysis, according to a [paper index/abstract](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2611.25584). Multi-omics fusion means combining molecular measurements from different technology platforms into a single model. HIT-Fuse stands out by being able to reconstruct missing data modalities while keeping traceable attribution paths. In a glioma cohort, including reconstructed data boosted the weighted F1 score by 56.4%. These are evaluation results on four TCGA cancer cohorts and haven't been turned into a clinical diagnostic tool yet.

### 7. Headaches Linked to Higher Risk of Hearing Loss in Later Life

Adults who reported having headaches had about a 70% higher risk of self-reported hearing impairment, according to a study analyzing over 91,000 health survey records from China and South Korea, as reported by [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261119/Headaches-linked-to-7025-higher-odds-of-hearing-loss-in-later-life.aspx). Longitudinal data from the Chinese cohort suggests headaches may be a potential indicator of hearing decline, but this is correlational evidence. It can't establish that headaches cause hearing loss, and shouldn't be used for clinical screening.

### 8. Cardiovascular Disease Patients Face Higher Mortality Risk During Summer Heatwaves

Cardiovascular disease patients who experienced higher long-term average summer temperatures or more heatwave events had a higher mortality risk, according to a UK analysis of health records covering over 60,000 cardiovascular disease patients spanning 16 years, as reported by [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261119/Summer-heatwaves-raise-death-risk-for-heart-disease-patients.aspx). This is an observational study confirming an association rather than causation, and differences in regional climate and healthcare conditions may affect how broadly the findings apply.

## 🧬 **Cutting-Edge Research**

### Comparison of Missing Data Handling Methods in Alzheimer's Research

Missing data is a widespread problem in Alzheimer's disease research datasets because biomarker collection through cerebrospinal fluid testing is costly and invasive. A [paper index for this preprint](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2611.25219) systematically evaluated 8 methods for filling in missing data, including traditional statistical methods, machine learning, and deep learning (variational autoencoders, generative adversarial networks, etc.), comparing estimation bias and computational burden at different missingness ratios through simulation experiments, and validating with real data from the Vanderbilt Memory and Aging Project. The study offers practical recommendations for method selection and is a useful reference for life science researchers dealing with incomplete longitudinal data. The paper is a preprint and hasn't been peer-reviewed yet.

### Thorne's AI Health Advisor Taia: Personalized Recommendations and Actual Health Improvements Are Two Different Things

Taia, an AI health advisor from supplement company Thorne, provides lifestyle and supplement recommendations based on users' biological data and health history, as reported by [Longevity.Technology](https://longevity.technology/news/when-the-best-answer-is-not-a-product/). Nathan Price, Thorne's Chief Scientific Officer (who also works at the Buck Institute for Research on Aging), introduced the system. The core takeaway from the report: getting users engaged, getting them to stick with it, and actually improving their health are three distinct things. This distinction matters for consumers evaluating such products. Whether a system can boost adherence and whether it's clinically proven to improve health outcomes are questions that need to be checked separately.
