## **🔥 Today's Top 8 Picks**

### 1. Longevity Supplement RCT Reports Major Vascular Function Gains

A randomized, double-blind controlled trial out of the University of Surrey, published in the *European Heart Journal Open*, tested the compound supplement NOVOS Core on vascular health. Vascular endothelial function was measured using flow-mediated dilation (FMD) — essentially how much an upper-arm artery expands when blood flow resumes, with higher FMD meaning more elastic vessels. Among the 43 participants who completed the six-month observation period, the supplement group's FMD climbed from roughly 5.5% to 8.1%, a jump of about 2.6 percentage points; the placebo group barely moved. Systolic blood pressure dropped 8.5 mmHg in the supplement group versus just 1.5 mmHg in controls. Arterial stiffness (pulse wave velocity) also improved. Lipid panels showed no significant between-group differences. The study has real limitations: nearly a third of the 61 enrolled participants didn't finish the follow-up, and the control group averaged about seven years older than the supplement group — a meaningful confound in a trial studying vascular aging. NOVOS co-funded the study and supplied the supplements, and the trial tested the full formulation rather than any single ingredient. According to [reporting from Lifespan.io](https://lifespan.io/longevity-supplement-improved-blood-vessels-in-a-human-trial/), independent reviewers found the effect size surprisingly large and are calling for independent replication. 🧪

### 2. Alzheimer's Disease May Be Shrinking Key Brain Regions a Decade Before Memory Loss

Johns Hopkins scientists found that two brain regions involved in memory and emotional processing may start shrinking years before any memory impairment appears — and that this happens earlier than the hippocampus (the brain's core hub for forming new memories) that doctors typically look at first. These early changes correlate with abnormal tau protein buildup, one of Alzheimer's hallmark pathological markers. If follow-up research confirms this, it could offer imaging-based clues for identifying high-risk individuals much earlier. Current coverage comes from [a summary-level Science Daily report](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261114202130.htm) — sample size, study design, and full data are pending review of the original paper. 🧠

### 3. Stanford Study: Hidden Brain Activity Patterns May Predict Alzheimer's Risk

Stanford researchers identified hidden patterns in neural activity that reportedly predict Alzheimer's risk before memory problems surface, and these patterns also appear linked to the brain's resilience against aging. If this holds up, it could provide an earlier warning window than current biomarkers offer. Only [a summary-level Science Daily report](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261112230444.htm) is available right now — which brain signals specifically, the sample size, and the observation timeframe all need to be verified against the original paper before any real assessment of predictive power is possible.

### 4. Daily Coffee and Dementia Risk: A 43-Year Observational Study

A 43-year observational study found that drinking two to three cups of caffeinated coffee per day correlates with lower dementia risk and better cognitive function, and this association showed up across different genetic risk backgrounds. Decaf showed no comparable association. Worth noting: observational research can only establish correlation, not that caffeine causally reduces dementia risk — other lifestyle factors can't be ruled out. The abstract is available via [Science Daily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261105224550.htm); full design and sample size are pending the original paper. ☕

### 5. Consistent Daily Routines Linked to Less Pain and Depression in Older Adults

University of Missouri researchers found that older adults who stick to fixed wake-up times, meal times, and daily activity schedules report less physical pain and fewer depression symptoms — and this association held even when insomnia was present. Regular routines are thought to stabilize the body's internal clock (circadian rhythm), and circadian disruption is closely tied to mood, sleep, and overall health. This is observational work showing correlation, not causation. Full details are in [the Science Daily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261113231624.htm), with sample and design specifics pending the original paper. 🕐

## **⌘ Top Open Source Projects**

### Gladyshev-Lab/tAge: An R Package for Estimating Biological Age from Transcriptomic Data

[tAge](https://github.com/Gladyshev-Lab/tAge) is an R package for transcriptomic biological age analysis, maintained by the Gladyshev Lab at Harvard University. The transcriptome is a snapshot of all genes actively expressed in a cell at a given moment — tAge uses this data to estimate a sample's biological age (functional aging relative to chronological age). It's a good fit for researchers with R experience who need to calculate biological age metrics on gene expression datasets. The repo was last active in November 2026 and has 30 stars. License details and specific input format requirements are on the [GitHub repo](https://github.com/Gladyshev-Lab/tAge). ⭐

### msbCyricTohoku/BACalc: A Dataset-Agnostic Dynamic Biological Age Calculator

[BACalc](https://github.com/msbCyricTohoku/BACalc) offers a biological age calculation method that isn't tied to any specific dataset — it's designed to work across health data from different sources without needing to be retrained for each new dataset. That makes it potentially useful for researchers doing cross-cohort biological age comparisons. The repo was last active in early November 2026 and has 1 star. README describes the functionality; actual performance and supported input formats are on the [GitHub repo](https://github.com/msbCyricTohoku/BACalc), with license status still to be confirmed.
