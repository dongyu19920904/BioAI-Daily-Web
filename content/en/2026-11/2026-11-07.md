## **🔥 Top 10 Today**

### 1. NOVOS Core, an anti-aging supplement, improved arterial function in a randomized controlled trial

As [reported by Lifespan.io](https://lifespan.io/longevity-supplement-improved-blood-vessels-in-a-human-trial/), NOVOS Core — a multi-ingredient supplement — was tested by a University of Surrey research team in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. 43 participants completed the primary analysis (24 in the supplement group, 19 in the placebo group). Flow-mediated dilation (FMD — how much an arm artery expands after blood flow is restored, reflecting blood vessel wall elasticity) improved by about 2.6 percentage points in the supplement group after six months, while the placebo group saw almost no change. Systolic blood pressure dropped by roughly 8.5 mmHg, with an adjusted between-group difference of 6.1 mmHg. Two major caveats worth noting: nearly a third of participants dropped out, the placebo group was about seven years older on average than the supplement group, and NOVOS both supplied the supplements and co-funded the study. It's also unclear which ingredient is doing the work — treat these results cautiously until an independent team replicates them.

### 2. A new epigenetics review argues aging probably has multiple rate-limiting steps, not one root cause

As [reported by Longevity.Technology](https://longevity.technology/news/epigenetics-may-be-agings-biggest-speed-limit/), a review published in the journal *Aging* introduces a "polycentric aging framework," arguing that aging is driven by several relatively independent processes rather than a single mechanism. Epigenetic regulation — the system that switches genes on and off without changing the DNA sequence itself — is considered by the authors to be the most broadly coordinating layer, though not the only rate-limiting one. The review also points out that existing epigenetic clocks (tools that estimate biological age from chemical markers on DNA) may not distinguish between harmful changes and compensatory ones — in the CALERIE dietary restriction trial and the DO-HEALTH fish oil trial, different clocks gave inconsistent readings. This is a conceptual review, not an experimental study, and the authors explicitly call for longitudinal research and controlled interventions. 🧬

### 3. FGF13, a protein in brain astrocytes, can trigger or slow whole-body aging in mice when its levels are changed

As [reported by Lifespan.io](https://lifespan.io/an-astrocytic-factor-slows-whole-body-aging-in-mice/), a study published in *Aging Cell* found that boosting expression of FGF13 — a protein in astrocytes, the brain cells that support and protect neurons — brought aging mice's spatial memory test performance close to that of young mice. Blood levels of inflammatory signaling molecules dropped significantly, and cellular senescence in multiple organs (outside the heart) was reduced. Since FGF13 can't be secreted out of cells, the researchers think its systemic effect is mediated through autophagy — the mechanism cells use to clear out old, damaged components. Results are currently mouse-only; non-human primate validation is needed before any human application, and the paper doesn't propose any specific delivery route. 🐭

### 4. Voyager Therapeutics is debuting two Alzheimer's tau-targeting therapies at a clinical conference in November

As [reported by Longevity.Technology](https://longevity.technology/news/voyager-readies-two-pronged-attack-on-alzheimers-tau/), Voyager Therapeutics will present two approaches targeting tau — the protein that forms tangles in the brain cells of Alzheimer's patients — at the November CTAD conference. VY1706 is a gene therapy designed to cross the blood-brain barrier after IV injection and suppress tau production directly in the brain; it's still in the study design phase with no human dosing yet. VY7523 is an antibody targeting existing tau protein, currently in the baseline enrollment phase of a Phase 1/2 dose-escalation trial, with no efficacy data whatsoever. Both posters report zero outcome numbers — this announcement is essentially a public declaration of research starting points. Whether tau therapies actually work remains an open question for every approach out there.

### 5. Dog Aging Project data from 41,000 companion dogs shows dog lifespans track human mortality by state — after controlling for body size

As [reported by Longevity.Technology](https://longevity.technology/news/the-lie-of-the-land-dog-lifespans-track-human-mortality-by-state/), the Dog Aging Project analyzed survival data from over 41,000 dogs published in *GeroScience*. After adjusting for size, breed, and sex, state-level canine mortality rates showed a significant positive correlation with human mortality rates — Tennessee, Oklahoma, and Kentucky were on the high end, while Hawaii and California came in low. Interestingly, what looked like a "rural penalty" disappeared once you accounted for rural dogs simply being larger on average — a neat demonstration of how easy it is to confuse environment with demographics. This is an ecological correlation across 51 state-level data points, not an individual causal analysis. The next phase will link environmental data like air quality and chemical exposure to individual dogs to hunt for shared factors. 🐕

### 6. A 43-year observational study on coffee and cognitive health has some new findings

As [reported by Science Daily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261105224550.htm), a 43-year observational study found that drinking 2–3 cups of caffeinated coffee daily was associated with significantly lower dementia risk and better cognitive function — and this association showed up across people with different genetic risk profiles. Decaf coffee didn't show the same association. The source only provides abstract-level information; specific sample size, journal, and institution still need to be verified. Observational research can't confirm causation — correlation doesn't mean coffee is directly reducing dementia risk. ☕

### 10. Sironax's neuroprotective drug SIR2501 reports early safety signals in ALS and chemo-induced nerve damage trials

As [reported by Longevity.Technology](https://longevity.technology/news/the-nerve-switch-sironax-wants-to-keep-switched-off/), SIR2501 from Sironax is a SARM1 inhibitor — SARM1 being the key enzyme that drives nerve fiber degeneration. The drug works by binding to an inactive site on the enzyme to keep it switched off, aiming to avoid the side effects that might come from directly blocking the active site. A poster at the November Neuroscience Annual Meeting reported "favorable safety and early bioactivity signals" in ongoing Phase 1/2 trials for ALS and chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (the numbness and pain in hands and feet that follows chemo). However, the announcement didn't include patient numbers, dosing details, or any efficacy metrics — safety and efficacy remain unestablished.

## **❓ Related Questions**

### Do the NOVOS Core trial results actually prove this supplement extends lifespan?

Short answer: nope. [The trial](https://lifespan.io/longevity-supplement-improved-blood-vessels-in-a-human-trial/) measured vascular function markers — arterial dilation and blood pressure — which are associated with cardiovascular health, but improved markers don't prove a reduction in heart attacks or extended lifespan. On top of that, the trial was small (just 43 people in the primary analysis), nearly a third of participants dropped out, the two groups had an average age difference of about seven years, and the study was co-funded by the manufacturer. Both the researchers themselves and outside commentators have explicitly said independent replication is needed before anyone can draw robust conclusions. ⚠️
