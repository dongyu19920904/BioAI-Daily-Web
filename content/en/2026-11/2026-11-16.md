## **🔥 Top 10 Today**

### 1. Inhibiting One Enzyme in Late Life Extended Median Mouse Lifespan by Four Months

Starting at 18 months of age (roughly equivalent to a human in their 50s), mice were given inhibitor #11a, which targets asparagine endopeptidase (AEP — a protease that becomes overactive in acidic environments and accumulates abnormally with age). Median survival jumped from 24 to 28 months, alongside drops in frailty index scores, slower cognitive and physical decline, and lower senescence cell markers across multiple organs. The effect size is comparable to late-life rapamycin use, and researchers think the two work through different pathways that both converge on mitochondrial function improvement. The paper is covered at [Fight Aging!](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/late-life-asparagine-endopeptidase-inhibition-extends-lifespan-in-mice/) — current evidence is mouse-only, the mechanism is still correlational, and direct extrapolation to humans isn't warranted. 🐭

### 2. CRISPR's Off-Target Problem Is Worse Than Most Researchers Expected

Superb-seq, a tool developed at the Salk Institute, can simultaneously read CRISPR/Cas9 edit sites and gene activity changes at the single-cell level — solving the previous inability to see both "where the edit landed" and "what happened to the cell as a result." Testing on roughly 10,000 human leukemia cells using 7 guide RNAs targeting 7 genes turned up 43 edit sites, 36 of which were off-target, affecting 5,761 cells total. The paper ran in Nature Methods, and per [GEN's coverage](https://www.genengnews.com/topics/genome-editing/single-cell-method-reveals-on-and-off-target-effects-of-cas9-edits-on-gene-expression/), researchers see this tool as useful for early safety screening of gene therapy candidates — though testing has been limited to cell lines and hasn't been validated on clinical samples yet. 🔬

### 3. Anti-Fibrotic Drug Rentosertib Consistently "Reversed" Six Proteomic Aging Clocks in Human Trials

Proteomic aging clocks estimate biological age by measuring the combined concentrations of hundreds of proteins in blood. Researchers analyzed serum samples from a 12-week Phase II clinical trial by Insilico Medicine (originally targeting idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis) using six different proteomic clocks simultaneously. The treatment group showed consistently lower estimated biological ages than the control group, with pathway analysis pointing toward reduced senescent cells and metabolic improvement. This is one of the rare cases where multiple aging clocks gave consistent signals in human trial data — see [Fight Aging!](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/anti-fibrotic-drug-rentosertib-reduces-proteomic-clock-age-in-humans/) for details. The clocks can't yet separate anti-aging effects from anti-fibrotic effects since the two mechanistically overlap, so longer follow-up and better-controlled trial designs are still needed. ⏳

### 4. An Alzheimer's Mouse Model Reveals the Full Cascade of Cerebrospinal Fluid Circulation Collapse

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulating through the brain is a key pathway for clearing metabolic waste — including amyloid proteins linked to Alzheimer's disease — in a system known as the glymphatic system. Using high-resolution imaging in Alzheimer's mouse models, researchers found structural collapse in the fluid channels surrounding large arteries at the base of the brain, preventing CSF from entering brain tissue normally. Meanwhile, cholesterol that healthy brains clear within 120 minutes was found accumulating around microvascular cells and neurons in the diseased models. The study proposes abnormal cholesterol metabolism as one driver of fluid circulation breakdown. Trace the original paper through [Fight Aging!](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/evidence-for-cerebrospinal-fluid-flow-through-the-brain-to-stagnate-in-alzheimers-disease/) — this is animal research. 🧠

### 5. Skin Cell Research Pinpoints Exactly Where Aging Breaks the Cell's Self-Cleaning System

Autophagy — the process by which cells send damaged proteins and structures to lysosomes for breakdown and recycling — gets weaker with age. Researchers analyzed autophagic flux in skin fibroblasts from donors aged 20 to 96 and found that the initiation stage functions roughly normally in aged cells, but autophagosome maturation and autophagosome-lysosome fusion both show defects in later steps. Cells from 96-year-old donors also displayed enlarged, dysfunctional lysosomes with accumulated waste. The main methodological contribution here is measuring each step separately to actually locate the failure point — something previous work hadn't done cleanly. The paper is in [Frontiers in Aging](https://doi.org/10.3389/fragi.2026.1971981); conclusions come from cell experiments and don't represent whole-body aging.

### 6. A Plant Extract Confirmed to Have Senolytic Activity — It Actually Clears Senescent Cells

Senescent cells — cells that can no longer divide normally but keep pumping out inflammatory signals — accumulate in aging tissue and damage surrounding structures. Through high-throughput screening, researchers identified cepharanthine (CEP), extracted from *Stephania* plants, as having selective senolytic activity. Reductions in senescent cells, improved organ function, and extended lifespan were observed across zebrafish, nematodes, naturally aged mice, and progeria mouse models. See [Fight Aging!](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/cepharanthine-identified-as-a-novel-senolytic/) for details. No human trial data exists yet, and getting a low-cost natural compound into large-scale clinical trials faces real funding barriers. 🌿

### 8. Senescent Cells Triggered by Cancer Treatment Are Accelerating Patients' Biological Aging

Therapy-induced senescence (TIS) happens when chemo and radiation fail to kill cancer cells outright and instead push them into a senescent state — no longer dividing, but still alive and secreting inflammatory signals. Normal surrounding cells get pushed into senescence too. This leaves cancer survivors facing higher rates of cardiovascular damage, chronic inflammation, and other age-related conditions compared to healthy peers of the same age. Reviewers flag the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) as a key mechanism behind cardiovascular toxicity, and suggest that combining senolytics with cancer treatment could reduce long-term side effects — though clinical benefit and safety haven't been established yet. Details at [Fight Aging!](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/the-double-edged-sword-of-therapy-induced-senescence-in-cancer-treatment-2/).

### 9. Speech Rhythm Features Reliably Flag Cognitive Impairment Across Four Languages

This preprint spans Spanish, Mandarin, English, and Japanese, collecting acoustic features through both conversational tasks and structured reading tasks, then using machine learning to identify which acoustic markers distinguish cognitive impairment. Speech timing features held up across all languages and tasks, capturing the pauses and rhythm disruptions characteristic of cognitive impairment. Speech rate was most reliable in reading tasks where external content anchors the speaker, but dropped off in free conversation. Pitch-related features showed different patterns in tonal languages (like Mandarin) versus non-tonal ones. The [paper](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2611.17710) hasn't been peer-reviewed yet and isn't ready to directly inform clinical screening tool development. 🎙️

### 10. Estrogen Exposure Duration Correlates with Reduced Neurodegeneration in 70-Year-Old Women

This study from the Gothenburg H70-1944 birth cohort examined 352 cognitively normal 70-year-old women with brain MRI at baseline and again 5 years later, analyzing associations between reproductive lifespan (a proxy for endogenous estrogen duration), hormone therapy use, and brain structure. Longer reproductive lifespan correlated with lower cerebral small vessel disease burden (odds ratio 0.92). Menopausal hormone therapy use was associated with fewer cerebral microvascular lesions and better white matter integrity. Among women carrying the APOE-ε4 allele (an Alzheimer's risk gene), hormone therapy was additionally associated with thicker cortical regions linked to Alzheimer's pathology. Full coverage at [Fight Aging!](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/longer-estrogen-exposure-correlates-with-a-lesser-degree-of-neurodegeneration/) — this is observational research, causation can't be inferred, and it doesn't constitute a hormone therapy recommendation.

## **⚡ Product & Feature Updates**

### AG-JEPA: Brain MRI Pre-Training Model Uses Anatomical Region Guidance to Improve Early Alzheimer's Detection

AG-JEPA is a proposed pre-training architecture for learning neurodegeneration representations from brain MRI, introduced in this preprint. Compared to general structural representation models, AG-JEPA incorporates anatomical tokens tied to Alzheimer's-relevant brain regions during pre-training, nudging the model to weight those regions more heavily. Across five downstream task benchmarks, AG-JEPA improved AUROC by 8.4 percentage points on cognitively normal vs. mild cognitive impairment (MCI — the transitional stage between normal aging and dementia) classification, and by 7.1 points on cross-dataset cognitively normal vs. Alzheimer's classification. The extended Prog-AG-JEPA variant can also model disease progression trajectories and predict future brain volume changes. Dig into the methods at the [paper index/abstract](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2611.18097) — this is a preprint, and all tests are classification benchmarks rather than clinical diagnostic validation.

## **🧪 Frontier Research**

### Mining Toxicology Archives Turned Up 27 Substances That Extend Mouse Lifespan

Researchers repurposed long-term rodent toxicology archives from Germany's Federal Institute for Risk Assessment — data originally generated for chemical safety approvals — and searched for compounds where low-dose groups showed improved survival rates. Of 154 substances with studies meeting the inclusion criteria (≥50 animals per group), 27 (18%) significantly improved endpoint survival in at least one dose group. Bromoxynil was confirmed as a mitochondrial uncoupler and further validated in *C. elegans*, where it improved multiple health indicators without detectable toxicity. The research approach is covered at [Fight Aging!](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/identifying-hormetic-agents-that-modestly-slow-aging-from-mouse-toxicology-study-data/) — all conclusions are from animal experiments, bromoxynil is a pesticide, human safety is unknown, and no human effects can be inferred. 🧫

## **❓ Related Questions**

### If a Proteomic Clock Shows a "Lower Biological Age," Does That Mean Someone Actually Got Younger?

Not really. Proteomic clocks (proteomic clocks) estimate biological age by measuring a combination of protein concentrations in blood — they're statistical model outputs, not direct measurements of body state. The fact that six clocks consistently pointed to lower estimated values in the Rentosertib treatment group tells us that certain aging-related protein signals shifted, but researchers themselves note the clocks currently can't distinguish "anti-aging effect" from "anti-fibrotic effect," since both influence overlapping sets of proteins. Trace the original paper through [Fight Aging!](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/anti-fibrotic-drug-rentosertib-reduces-proteomic-clock-age-in-humans/). A lower estimated value doesn't equal reduced disease risk, and it doesn't equal longer lifespan — a randomized controlled trial with longer follow-up is what's needed to answer questions about functional outcomes. 🤔
