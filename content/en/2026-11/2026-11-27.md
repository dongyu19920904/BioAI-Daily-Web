## **🔥 Today's Top 6 Highlights**

### 1. Blue Zone longevity advantage gets remeasured — and it's down to four months

Researchers built a machine-learning-based epigenetic clock for residents of Costa Rica's Nicoya Blue Zone — a region famous for its high centenarian ratio — using DNA methylation patterns to estimate biological age rather than chronological age. Before correcting for prediction bias, Nicoya residents appeared biologically about two years younger than their actual age. But the team found that these clocks have systematic accuracy gaps across different age groups, and after adjusting for reference population and age distribution, the advantage collapsed to roughly four months. As covered in [News-Medical's report](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261126/Do-people-in-blue-zones-really-age-more-slowly.aspx), researchers noted that "selective survival" — where healthier people live longer and thus stay in the sample — may partly explain the remaining gap. The jury's still out on whether Blue Zone residents actually age more slowly. 🔍

### 2. Slowly rising blood sugar in your 20s? Your brain at 55 might feel it

In a 30-year study tracking 2,808 adults, researchers found that even when fasting blood sugar stays below the diabetes diagnosis threshold, a slow, steady climb starting in young adulthood is linked to worse memory, executive function, and information processing scores by middle age. Participants who eventually developed diabetes also showed imaging changes: reduced gray matter volume (the neuron-dense layer responsible for thinking) and more white matter abnormalities. As [News-Medical reports](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261126/What-rising-blood-sugar-in-your-20s-could-mean-for-your-brain-at-55.aspx), this is an observational study — it shows an association between blood sugar trajectory and brain health, but it doesn't prove causation. 🧠

### 3. One NAD+-boosting method damages bone in mice — and the mechanism isn't clear yet

NAD+ is a key coenzyme in cellular energy metabolism that declines with age, and boosting it is widely considered beneficial across multiple systems. Researchers compared mice with the CD38 gene knocked out (CD38 is one of the main NAD+-consuming proteins in the body) against normal mice. The knockout mice did show higher NAD+ levels in bone, but as they aged, their bone thickness was actually lower and their leg bone porosity was higher. Further analysis showed that osteoclast activity (the cells that break down bone) outpaced osteoblast compensation. By contrast, mice overexpressing Nampt — a different NAD+-boosting pathway — didn't show the same bone damage. As [Lifespan.io reports](https://lifespan.io/one-method-of-increasing-nad-harms-bone-in-mice/), researchers haven't yet pinned down the specific mechanism of CD38 in osteoclasts, and all results come from mice — extrapolating to humans isn't warranted. 🦴

### 4. Blood NAD+ stays stable with age in healthy people — but drops when hypertension enters the picture

A research team in Vilnius, Lithuania collected 1,340 fingertip blood samples from 1,068 adults and measured whole-blood NAD+ levels. In healthy individuals, blood NAD+ doesn't decline with age: it stays stable in men and actually trends slightly upward in women. But in people with hypertension, NAD+ levels were meaningfully lower in both sexes. As [Longevity.Technology reports](https://longevity.technology/news/blood-nad-keeps-time-with-the-heart-not-the-calendar/), this is observational research — it shows NAD+ correlates with cardiovascular status, but it can't tell us which is cause and which is effect, and it doesn't guide whether NAD+ precursor supplementation would actually help. ❤️

### 5. Hemostemix reports heart pumping improvements in Phase 1 stem cell trial

Hemostemix, a Canadian biotech, presented Phase 1 data for its experimental stem cell therapy at an investor conference on November 19th. The numbers showed left ventricular shrinkage (the enlarged left ventricle being a consequence of heart attack-related muscle damage), rising ejection fraction (the metric measuring what fraction of blood the heart pumps per beat), and increased six-minute walk distance in patients. As [Longevity.Technology reports](https://longevity.technology/news/hemostemix-reports-improved-heart-function-with-stem-cells/), this Phase 1 trial had no control group and was primarily designed to assess safety — it's too early to confirm efficacy or calculate what proportion of enrolled patients completed the full treatment course.

### 6. Review of nine exercise types and their potential effects on aging bones

[News-Medical covers](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261127/From-lifting-weights-to-Tai-Chi-which-exercises-help-aging-bones.aspx) a review examining nine exercise types for their potential bone density benefits in older adults. The review found stronger supporting evidence for approaches like resistance training, while the relationship between Tai Chi and fracture prevention remains uncertain. Since only the headline and a short abstract were available, specific sample sizes, exercise intensity details, and effect size data are pending verification. 🏋️

## **◎ Industry Shifts & What They Mean for You**

### The longevity wellness industry is pivoting to measurable biomarkers

Cooper Adwin of design consultancy Designerly, as covered by [Longevity.Technology](https://longevity.technology/news/when-wellness-has-to-show-its-working/), points out that consumer and investor expectations for health products are shifting from "feeling better" to quantifiable proof — think clinically validated biological age clocks and continuous biomarker monitoring. For startups, that means brand storytelling alone no longer holds up as a fundraising thesis; verifiable health outcome data is the new ante. Worth noting: this piece is industry commentary from a company consultant, not independent academic research. 📊
