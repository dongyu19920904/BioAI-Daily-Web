### 1. Vanadyl Sulfate Supplement Clears Senescent Cells and Extends Lifespan in Mice

As [reported by Lifespan.io](https://lifespan.io/a-supplement-kills-senescent-cells-and-extends-life-in-mice/), vanadyl sulfate (VS) — a mineral compound found in shellfish, mushrooms, and soybeans, also sold as a dietary supplement — is at the center of a new study published in *Nature Aging* by a University of Chicago research team. 🧫 Senescent cells are cells that stop dividing but don't die off; instead, they just sit there pumping out harmful inflammatory signals that accelerate aging. The team added VS to the drinking water of male mice around 71 weeks old (roughly elderly by mouse standards) for 90 days. Median lifespan jumped from 887 to 952 days — a 7.4% bump — while body fat dropped, physical performance and blood sugar regulation improved, and kidney inflammation markers declined. Mechanistically, VS appears to kill senescent cells by promoting abnormal protein misfolding, while healthy cells survive because they have stronger repair machinery. ⚠️ Worth flagging: each group had about 20 mice, the lifespan extension barely crossed the statistical significance threshold, female mice weren't tested for longevity, and this is still animal data — whether any of this translates to humans is totally unknown.

### 2. Bonbon, an AI Foundation Model, Shows Zero-Shot Emergent Capabilities in Drug Discovery

Bonbon is a foundation model detailed in this [paper index/abstract](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2611.14504), trained on roughly 3.2 trillion protein-ligand sequence pairs — where ligands are small molecules that bind to proteins and could become drugs. 🤯 What's wild is that the model was never explicitly taught any specific task during training, yet it spontaneously developed three "emergent" abilities: identifying drug mechanisms of action, locating binding sites on proteins, and distinguishing covalent from non-covalent binding. In wet-lab validation across 9 disease targets, 61.5% of Bonbon's novel compounds showed activity in dose-response experiments, including entirely new structural scaffolds previously unreported as inhibitors of those proteins. This is preprint-stage stuff — not peer-reviewed yet — and lab activity is a long way from an actual clinical drug.

### 3. Chemotherapy May Accelerate Biological Aging in Breast Cancer Survivors

Chemotherapy gets a closer look in [this News-Medical report](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261110/Chemotherapy-may-accelerate-biological-aging-in-breast-cancer-survivors.aspx): new research suggests that while chemo helps breast cancer patients survive, it may push the body's "biological age" — an estimate of how old your body actually is based on blood or cellular markers, distinct from your birth year — ahead of chronological age. 🧬 The study also suggests this could raise patients' risk of dying from other diseases down the line. ### 4. Pyrite: An AI Agent for Ranking Molecular Binding Poses in Drug Design

Pyrite is a new benchmark introduced in this [paper index/abstract](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2611.14511), specifically designed to evaluate "ligand binding pose ranking" — a task where the AI judges which molecular configuration of a drug candidate is most likely to actually bind a target protein. The benchmark covers 1,500 binding pose pairs. 😅 Fascinatingly, expert chemists equipped with 3D visualization tools performed near chance level, showing just how genuinely hard this problem is. Building on this, the researchers developed two LLM-based agents: PyriteReasoner (direct text-based reasoning) hits 67% accuracy, while PyriteFeaturizer (which guides the model to auto-generate feature extraction code) reaches 74% — both beating human expert performance. This is preprint-stage benchmark research; accuracy here reflects performance on the benchmark set, not real-world drug development success rates.

### 5. Foundation Model Adaptation Beats Specialist Models for Cross-Institutional Medical Image Segmentation

Foundation models like DINOv2 — general-purpose vision models pre-trained on large-scale natural images — are the focus of this [paper index/abstract](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2611.14519), which systematically compares adapting them to medical image segmentation tasks. 🏥 The study spans colonoscopy, breast ultrasound, and dermoscopy, and on external test sets from different institutions, adapted foundation models outperformed task-specific models trained from scratch in generalization performance across every dataset. That said, when training data is plentiful, specialist models are more precise at delineating lesion boundaries and have lower small-lesion miss rates. The researchers also found that very few model parameters need adapting, and the right choice depends on the imaging domain rather than the adaptation method. Preprint — not yet peer-reviewed.

### 6. DrugCoding: An AI Coding Agent for Automating Multi-Stage Drug Discovery Pipelines

DrugCoding is the system proposed in this [paper index/abstract](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2611.13651) — an AI that autonomously plans tasks, calls specialized scientific models, and generates executable code to chain together molecular design, property prediction, and virtual screening into an automated drug discovery pipeline. ⚙️ Across multi-round tool-calling evaluations covering various language models and scientific tool interfaces, DrugCoding hits a Pass@1 rate (meaning it nails the task correctly on the first try) of 97.35% on the full benchmark, beating the strongest baseline by 9.24 percentage points. These results are on a standardized benchmark — real drug discovery pipelines are far messier and more uncertain. Still preprint stage.

### 7. Predicting Single-Cell Spatial Gene Expression from Tissue Slide Images

This [paper index/abstract](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2611.13649) demonstrates predicting single-cell-level gene expression directly from H&E-stained pathology images — the routine tissue staining slides hospitals already make. 🔬 Trained with paired Xenium 5K spatial transcriptomics data and using a domain adaptation strategy, the method achieves a 2.67× improvement in average gene prediction correlation over the zero-shot baseline, reaches a correlation coefficient of 0.63 for spatially differentially expressed genes, and hits an AUROC of 0.80 for detecting all genes. The potential upside here is getting partial gene information without expensive molecular testing — but prediction performance varies significantly across biological contexts, and the association between H&E images and gene expression doesn't mean you can substitute one for the other. Preprint stage.

### 8. A Landmark Alzheimer's Longitudinal Study Lands $32M Grant Extended Through 2031

The Alzheimer's longitudinal research program — originally launched in the 1970s by neurologist Leonard Berg at Washington University in St. Louis — is the subject of [this News-Medical report](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261110/2432-million-grant-extends-landmark-Alzheimers-research-through-2031.aspx). 🧠 The project just secured a $32 million grant to continue through 2031. ### 9. Yoga Improves Mood and Memory After 45 Days, But the Long-Term Meaning of Brain Connectivity Changes Remains Unclear

A randomized controlled trial is the focus of [this News-Medical report](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261110/Scientists-scanned-peoples-brains-before-and-after-45-days-of-yoga-Heres-what-changed.aspx): 139 healthy adults participated in a 45-day standardized yoga program, and compared to a waitlist control group, participants showed improvements in mood, self-reported sleep quality, and some short-term memory measures. 🧘 Brain scans from 43 of those participants showed functional connectivity changes in networks involved in motor control, attention, language, and cognitive processing — where "functional connectivity" means how much different brain regions activate in sync, measurable by MRI. The researchers explicitly noted that the long-term significance of these connectivity changes is currently unknown, and you can't extrapolate from this that yoga prevents cognitive decline.

### 10. Ergothioneine in Mushrooms May Protect Against Neurodegeneration, But Human Evidence Is Thin

Ergothioneine — a natural antioxidant found primarily in mushrooms — is the subject of a new review covered in [this News-Medical report](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261110/Could-eating-more-mushrooms-help-protect-your-brain-as-you-age.aspx). 🍄 Lab and animal studies show it has protective effects against oxidative stress and inflammation linked to Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease. But human evidence is still pretty sparse — the review authors say larger clinical trials are needed before anyone can conclude whether ergothioneine supplementation actually prevents or slows neurodegeneration. The existing results aren't a basis for recommending people load up on mushrooms just yet.

### Amyloid Accumulation in the Brain's Choroid Plexus Shows Nonlinear Patterns in Middle Age

The choroid plexus — the brain structure responsible for producing cerebrospinal fluid — is the subject of [this preprint](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/PPR1361149), which examines the formation of "Biondi body" amyloid deposits in humans. 🧬 The study finds that this process shows nonlinear changes across scales during middle age, with potential links to the early stages of neurodegenerative disease. The choroid plexus and amyloid accumulation are considered a potentially important early-window target in Alzheimer's research. This is an unreviewed preprint on human samples — specific sample size and methodology need to be verified in the original paper.
