### 1. Chronic Loneliness and Dementia Risk: A 30-Year Study Finds a Key Distinction

According to [a report by Lifespan.io](https://lifespan.io/chronic-loneliness-increases-dementia-risk/), a cohort study tracking over 1,500 adults aged 65+ for nearly 30 years found that people who felt lonely repeatedly over time had a 38% higher dementia risk than those who never reported loneliness. Those who felt lonely only occasionally, however, showed no equivalent risk increase after controlling for other factors. 🧠

Researchers measured loneliness multiple times over 8 years and classified anyone who reported it three or four times as "chronically lonely" — a distinction from those who reported it once or twice. That's a level of granularity most prior studies missed. Of the 560 participants who developed dementia during follow-up, the chronic loneliness group had a noticeably higher incidence rate (49.1%) versus those who were never lonely (33.1%).

This is an observational study, so it shows association, not direct causation. It also can't rule out that loneliness is an early symptom of cognitive decline rather than a contributing cause.

### 2. Avexitide Hits Primary Endpoint in Phase 3 Trial for Post-Bariatric Hypoglycemia

According to [a report by Longevity.Technology](https://longevity.technology/news/avexitide-cuts-post-bypass-sugar-crashes-by-55/), avexitide — a drug from Amylyx Pharmaceuticals — cut the frequency of post-meal blood sugar crashes after gastric bypass surgery by 55% in a Phase 3 trial called LUCIDITY. 💊

Post-bariatric hypoglycemia is a complication of gastric bypass where blood sugar drops sharply after eating. No FDA-approved treatment currently exists for it. The trial enrolled 78 adults, and results were presented at the 2026 Obesity Week conference. The company plans to file with the FDA before the end of the year.

Worth noting: 78 participants is a relatively small trial, and filing for approval isn't the same as getting it. Final market availability still depends on regulatory review.

### 5. BioScore: A New Benchmark for AI Pathology Models Based on Cell Identity, Not Task Accuracy

[Paper index/abstract](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2611.34930) introduces BioScore, a benchmark designed to test whether AI pathology models have actually learned cell biology — not just how to score well on classification tasks.

Most existing benchmarks measure accuracy on a specific task. BioScore instead uses spatial transcriptomics (a technology that records gene expression at every location in a tissue sample) as a reference, checking whether a model can identify 52 gene-defined cell types directly from images. Among 8 pathology-specific models and 3 general-purpose image models tested, all pathology models scored higher (0.299–0.371) than the general models (0.214–0.232), and three performance tiers emerged within the pathology group.

BioScore requires no manual annotations, which is a real practical advantage. That said, it's an evaluation tool — not a clinical diagnostic system. 🔬

### 6. Ultrasound Video Generation Model Adds Anatomical Priors to Fix Consistency After Probe Removal

[Paper index/abstract](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2611.34622) covers a new approach to ultrasound video generation. Traditional methods generate images based solely on probe position, so when the probe leaves an area and returns, the reconstructed anatomy is often inconsistent with what was shown before.

This work introduces two additions: anatomical priors built from population data (essentially statistical expectations of normal anatomy) to fill in unscanned regions, and a 3D latent memory that integrates already-generated slices into a volumetric model, keeping repeated scans of the same location consistent. The approach outperformed comparison methods on thyroid, kidney, and lumbar spine data in terms of both visual fidelity and anatomical coherence.

This is still algorithmic research — real-world clinical ultrasound guidance applications await further validation. 🏥

### 7. Sage-Med-Preview: A Medical Multimodal LLM Benchmarked Across 238 Clinical Tasks

[Paper index/abstract](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2611.34575) introduces Sage-Med-Preview, a medical multimodal large language model with 35 billion total parameters (3 billion active), trained on roughly 327 million candidate records through a staged filtering process.

Across 238 clinical tasks spanning 40+ imaging modalities, Sage-Med-Preview averaged 58.26% on 31 public multimodal benchmarks — beating the open-source medical model Hulu-Med-235B-A22B (50.99%) and the general science model Intern-S2-Preview (52.24%), and approaching GPT-6.2-Sol on some tasks. The paper itself flags significant remaining gaps in complex medical reasoning and multi-step clinical workflows. Benchmark scores don't equal clinical readiness. 🤖

## **⌘ Top Open-Source Projects**

### AndrewsLabUCSF/ADNI-BioAge: Estimating Biological Age in an Alzheimer's Cohort Using Epigenetic Clocks

This [open-source repository](https://github.com/AndrewsLabUCSF/ADNI-BioAge) from the UCSF Andrews Lab applies epigenetic clocks to data from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort to calculate biological age. Epigenetic clocks are algorithms that estimate how much a cell has actually aged by reading DNA methylation patterns (chemical modifications on genes) — the gap between that estimate and chronological age is used as an aging research metric.

It's a solid resource for researchers interested in how epigenetic clocks apply to dementia-related cohorts. The README hasn't been independently verified, the star count is low, and runtime environment and license status are worth checking on the repo page directly. 🧬
