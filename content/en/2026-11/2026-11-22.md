## **🔥 Today's Top 8 Highlights**

### 1. Nitroglycerin + Whole-Body Vibration May Flush Metabolic Waste from the Brain

The brain relies on a network called the glymphatic system to flush metabolic waste during sleep — a system that degrades with age, and whose failure is thought to contribute to Alzheimer's and other neurodegenerative diseases. [As reported by Fight Aging!](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/vasodilatory-and-vibratory-treatment-improves-glymphatic-drainage-in-humans/), a small human trial (EudraCT 2021-000625-27) in 29 healthy adults found that combining sublingual nitroglycerin (a vasodilator) with low-frequency whole-body vibration raised blood levels of brain-derived proteins Aβ40, Aβ42, and GFAP — suggesting waste briefly shifted into the bloodstream, with stronger effects in older participants and those with larger blood pressure drops. This pattern is consistent with improved glymphatic drainage, but the sample is tiny, there's no control group, and whether waste is actually cleared from the brain long-term remains unproven. 🧠

### 2. Aging-Related Protein Gas6 May Be Driving Aortic Valve Calcification to Spread

Aortic valve calcification — where heart tissue stiffens and the valve loses flexibility — is a deadly age-related condition with no good reversal therapy today. [The study covered by Fight Aging!](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/gas6-contributes-to-the-spread-of-calcification-in-the-aortic-valve/) found that Gas6, a protein secreted by senescent cells in the valve, triggers a signaling cascade that causes neighboring healthy cells to also start depositing calcium, spreading calcification outward from the original site. Knocking out Gas6 in mice reduced valve stenosis, calcification, and fibrosis. The researchers note that Gas6's two downstream protein targets do too much other important cardiac work to block safely, making Gas6 itself the more attractive upstream intervention point — though all findings are still in cells and mice. 🫀

### 3. Aging Clocks Like PhenoAge May Be Measuring Disease Burden, Not True Aging, in Cancer Patients

PhenoAge is a "biological age clock" that estimates aging from standard blood markers — things like red cell distribution width, albumin, and lymphocyte percentage. [This study](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/aging-clocks-may-reflect-disease-burden-in-addition-to-aging-in-many-circumstances/) analyzed data across four cancer types and found that while accelerated PhenoAge does correlate with worse outcomes, its values rise with tumor stage, and its predictive power comes mainly from one or two markers that vary by cancer type — markers that also reflect inflammation, nutritional status, and organ damage, not aging specifically. Swapping the original weights for simple substitutes barely changed predictive accuracy, suggesting PhenoAge is largely capturing the systemic effects of disease itself in these patients. The researchers are clear: this doesn't mean aging is irrelevant to cancer, just that oncology research needs aging metrics that can be disentangled from disease consequences. 🔬

### 4. Granzyme K Knockout Mice Age More Slowly — But Their Inflammation Looks Normal

Chronic low-grade inflammation is a hallmark of aging and is widely considered one of its core drivers. [The study covered by Fight Aging!](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/granzyme-k-knockout-mice-exhibit-reduced-loss-of-function-with-age/) found that mice lacking Granzyme K — a protease mainly produced by CD8+ T cells — showed significantly slower age-related organ function decline and lower blood levels of proteins reflecting cellular damage and organ stress. The surprise? Their inflammatory markers (IL-6, MCP-1) looked no different from normal aged mice. This unexpected result suggests Granzyme K accelerates aging locally in tissues through non-inflammatory pathways — not just by stoking inflammation. All conclusions are from mice so far. 🐭

### 5. The Brain's CSF Flushing Waves Weaken with Age

During sleep, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) generates large, low-frequency flow waves that sweep away the metabolic waste the brain accumulates during the day. [This study](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/cerebrospinal-fluid-flow-waves-decline-in-the-aging-brain/) compared nighttime sleep brain imaging in two healthy groups — ages 18–39 and 60–84 — and found that CSF flow wave amplitude was significantly lower in older adults, correlating with weaker EEG slow-wave power, reduced whole-brain hemodynamic oscillations, and gray matter atrophy. Using sensory and vasoactive stimulation to independently drive CSF flow, the researchers confirmed that both neural activity and vascular function are involved. These changes are observable before disease appears, but whether improving CSF flow can actually slow neurodegeneration remains an open question. 💤

### 6. Injecting Myc and Cyclin T1 mRNA into Mouse Hearts Triggers Regeneration After Heart Attack

Adult hearts are nearly incapable of self-repair — cardiomyocytes (the cells that make the heart contract) barely divide, and a heart attack typically leaves permanent scar tissue. [The study covered by Fight Aging!](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/overexpression-of-myc-and-cyclin-t1-via-mrna-therapy-improves-regeneration-of-heart-tissue/) found that co-expressing the gene regulators Myc and Cyclin T1 pushes mouse cardiomyocytes back into division, achieving tissue repair and functional recovery in a heart attack model. The researchers used mRNA injection for transient expression rather than permanent genetic modification. The current delivery method isn't ready for humans, and both safety and efficacy need validation before this moves beyond mice. ❤️‍🩹

### 7. Alzheimer's May Shrink Two Brain Regions a Decade Before Memory Loss

[A Johns Hopkins University study reported by Science Daily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261114202130.htm) found via brain scans that two regions involved in memory and emotion begin shrinking before the hippocampal atrophy typically looked for in clinical assessments — possibly as much as ten years before memory symptoms appear. These early changes were linked to abnormal tau protein accumulation, a direct marker of Alzheimer's-related neuronal damage. The finding suggests an earlier imaging warning window may exist, though the summary doesn't specify sample size, follow-up duration, or clinical validation, so its applicability to individual risk screening is still unclear. 🧬

### 8. Regular Daily Routines Are Linked to Less Pain and Depression in Older Adults

[A University of Missouri study reported by Science Daily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261113231624.htm) found that older adults who stick to consistent wake, meal, and activity times report less physical pain and fewer depression symptoms — even when they also have insomnia. The researchers think a fixed schedule helps maintain the biological rhythms that regulate mood, sleep, and overall health. This is observational research; the summary doesn't report sample size or follow-up length, and the association between routine and fewer symptoms doesn't establish causation — adjusting your schedule may not directly produce those benefits. 📅

## **🧪 Frontier Research**

### An mRNA Vaccine Revives Aged Dendritic Cells and Clears Senescent Fat Cells at the Same Time

Senescent cells — cells that have stopped dividing but keep pumping out inflammatory signals — accumulate in aging tissue and are a known driver of metabolic decline. [The preprint covered by Fight Aging!](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/a-senolytic-vaccine-that-also-improves-dendritic-cell-function/) describes a dual-adjuvant nanovaccine with two jobs: a positively charged spider-silk protein scaffold repairs the senescent state of local dendritic cells (the immune system's antigen-recognition specialists), restoring their ability to activate immune responses; and the vaccine trains CD8+ killer T cells to target and clear senescent fat cells expressing the Gpnmb protein. In multiple aged mouse models, vaccination cleared senescent cells from fat tissue, improved metabolic function, and extended lifespan. Hitting both the immune system and the target cells simultaneously is the methodological highlight here — but every result is still in mice. 💉

### Skin Fibroblast Study Pinpoints Exactly Where Cellular Garbage Collection Breaks Down with Age

Autophagy is the cell's self-cleaning system: it packages damaged proteins and structures, then delivers them to organelles called lysosomes for breakdown and recycling — a process critical to cell health. [This study](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/assessing-impaired-macroautophagy-in-aged-skin-cells/) used human skin fibroblasts from donors aged 20 to 96 and found that autophagy initiation and the initial "packaging" step remain largely intact in aged cells, but autophagosome maturation and fusion with lysosomes — the later steps — are impaired. Lysosomes themselves were visibly enlarged and functionally compromised in the oldest donors' cells, leading to waste buildup (lipofuscin accumulation). Knowing exactly which step fails should help design targeted interventions, though all findings are currently from cell culture. 🔭

### GLP-1 Prescriptions Are Rising, But Access Gaps Are Getting Wider

GLP-1 drugs — a class that mimics gut hormones to suppress appetite and lower blood sugar and body weight, including semaglutide — have seen rapid prescription growth in recent years. [This new study reported by News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261120/GLP-1-access-disparities-widen-despite-rising-prescriptions-for-weight-management.aspx) found that young adults, older adults, men, Black and Hispanic patients, people with lower BMI, and patients in lower socioeconomic communities are all still less likely to receive prescriptions. Overall prescription volume is up — but the gaps between groups are actually widening. The source is a short summary; sample size and study design should be verified against the full paper. 💊

## **⌘ Open Source Picks**

### Gladyshev-Lab/tAge: An R Package for Estimating Biological Age from Gene Expression Data

[tAge](https://github.com/Gladyshev-Lab/tAge) is an R package that takes transcriptomic data (measurements of how actively genes are expressed in cells) as input and outputs estimated biological age for each sample. Biological age is a molecular-state estimate relative to chronological age, commonly used to assess the effect of aging interventions. It's aimed at researchers with transcriptomics experience; the repo covers input formats and usage. Stars: 30, last active November 2026. Biological age estimates are statistical outputs — they can't be used directly for personal health diagnosis. 🧬

### msbCyricTohoku/BACalc: A Biological Age Calculator Built to Work Across Datasets

[BACalc](https://github.com/msbCyricTohoku/BACalc) is designed to run across datasets from different sources without needing recalibration for each one — useful for researchers comparing biological age across multiple cohorts. Supported data types and input formats are in the repo. Stars: 1, last active early November 2026; license status needs confirmation. 📊

## **❓ Related Questions**

### Can a PhenoAge Number Actually Tell You Whether You're Aging Faster Than Normal?

PhenoAge's component markers — albumin, lymphocyte percentage, and others — simultaneously reflect inflammation, nutrition, and organ load. Today's covered research showed that in cancer patients, it's hard to separate "accelerated aging" from "the systemic effects of disease itself." In healthy people this problem is smaller, but a single reading is still an estimate from a population-level statistical model and doesn't tell you whether your brain or any specific organ is aging faster. [The original analysis is available via Fight Aging!'s coverage](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/aging-clocks-may-reflect-disease-burden-in-addition-to-aging-in-many-circumstances/) — the study itself hasn't established whether its conclusions extend to disease-free healthy populations. 🤔
