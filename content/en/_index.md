## **🔥 Today's Top 6 Highlights**

### 1. Blue Zone longevity advantage gets remeasured — and it's down to four months

Researchers built a machine-learning-based epigenetic clock for residents of Costa Rica's Nicoya Blue Zone (a region known for its high centenarian rate) — using DNA methylation patterns to estimate biological age rather than chronological age. Before correcting for prediction bias, Nicoya residents appeared biologically about two years younger than their actual age. But the team found that these clocks have systematic accuracy gaps across different age groups, and once they adjusted for reference population and age distribution, the advantage collapsed to roughly four months. As covered by [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261126/Do-people-in-blue-zones-really-age-more-slowly.aspx), researchers noted that "selective survival" — where healthier people live longer and stay in the sample — may partly explain the remaining gap, so we still can't conclude that Blue Zone residents actually age more slowly. 🤔

![Blue Zone and biological age research](https://www.news-medical.net/images/news/ImageForNews_852886_17957382919706528.jpg)

### 2. Blood sugar creeping up in your 20s? Your brain at 55 might feel it 🧠

In a study tracking 2,808 adults over 30 years, researchers found that even when fasting blood sugar stays below the diabetes threshold, a slow and steady rise from a young age correlates with worse memory, executive function, and information processing speed by middle age. Participants who eventually developed diabetes also showed reduced gray matter volume (the brain layer housing neurons responsible for thinking) and more white matter abnormalities on imaging. As covered by [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261126/What-rising-blood-sugar-in-your-20s-could-mean-for-your-brain-at-55.aspx), this is an observational study — it shows an association between blood sugar trajectory and brain health, but doesn't directly prove causation.

### 3. One way to boost NAD+ damages bone in mice 🦴

NAD+, a key coenzyme in cellular energy metabolism, declines with age, and boosting it is widely thought to benefit multiple systems. Researchers compared mice with the CD38 gene knocked out (CD38 is one of the main proteins that depletes NAD+ in the body) against normal mice. NAD+ levels in the bones of knockout mice were indeed higher — but their bones were thinner in old age, with greater leg bone porosity. Further analysis showed osteoclast activity (the cells that break down bone) outpaced the compensatory rate of osteoblasts (the cells that build it). Meanwhile, mice overexpressing Nampt — a different NAD+-boosting pathway — didn't show the same bone damage. As reported by [Lifespan.io](https://lifespan.io/one-method-of-increasing-nad-harms-bone-in-mice/), researchers haven't pinned down exactly what CD38 does in osteoclasts, and all results come from mice, so this doesn't translate directly to humans.

### 4. Blood NAD+ stays stable with age — but drops when you have hypertension ❤️

A research team in Vilnius, Lithuania collected 1,340 fingertip blood samples from 1,068 adults and measured whole-blood NAD+ levels. In healthy individuals, blood NAD+ doesn't decline with age: it stays stable in men and actually trends slightly upward in women. Among people with hypertension, though, NAD+ levels were noticeably lower in both sexes. As covered by [Longevity.Technology](https://longevity.technology/news/blood-nad-keeps-time-with-the-heart-not-the-calendar/), this is an observational study — it shows a link between NAD+ and cardiovascular status, but can't tell us which direction the causality runs, or whether supplementing NAD+ precursors would actually help.

### 5. Hemostemix reports heart pumping improvements in Phase 1 stem cell trial 💉

Canadian biotech Hemostemix presented Phase 1 data at an investor meeting on November 19, showing that its experimental stem cell therapy produced a reduction in left ventricular enlargement (caused by heart muscle damage after a heart attack), an increase in ejection fraction (the measure of how much blood the heart pumps with each beat), and gains in patients' six-minute walk distance. As covered by [Longevity.Technology](https://longevity.technology/news/hemostemix-reports-improved-heart-function-with-stem-cells/), this Phase 1 trial had no control group and was primarily designed to assess safety — so it's too early to confirm efficacy or extrapolate the proportion of patients who completed the full course of treatment.

### 6. A look at nine types of exercise and what they might do for aging bones 🏋️

[News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261127/From-lifting-weights-to-Tai-Chi-which-exercises-help-aging-bones.aspx) covers a review assessing nine types of exercise for their potential benefits on bone density in older adults. Resistance training had the most supporting evidence, while the relationship between Tai Chi and fracture prevention remains unclear. Since the available material only includes a headline and short abstract, specific sample sizes, exercise intensity details, and effect size data are still pending verification.

## **🧪 Frontier Research**

### Immune aging in women living with HIV through menopause

As covered by [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261126/Researcher-explores-menopause-and-immune-health-in-women-aging-with-HIV.aspx), advances in antiretroviral therapy have significantly extended lifespans for women living with HIV — which puts a spotlight on a new research question: how does the intersection of menopause and immune aging play out when the immune system has already been shaped by long-term viral exposure? Specifically, how does declining estrogen alter an immune system already under that chronic burden? ## **◎ Industry Shifts & What They Mean for You**

### The longevity wellness industry is pivoting to measurable biomarkers 📊

Designerly consultant Cooper Adwin, as reported by [Longevity.Technology](https://longevity.technology/news/when-wellness-has-to-show-its-working/), points out that consumer and investor expectations for health products are shifting from "feeling better" toward quantifiable proof — think clinically validated biological age clocks and continuous biomarker monitoring. For startups, that means brand storytelling alone no longer holds up as a funding thesis; you need verifiable health outcome data. Worth noting: this piece is industry commentary, and the analysis comes from a company consultant rather than independent academic research.
