## **🔥 Today's Top 10 Hottest Stories**

### 1. Splicing Factor SRSF1 Extends Lifespan in Aged Mice by 14%

SRSF1 is the star of today's longevity news. 🧬 Researchers used machine learning to scan gene expression profiles of human skin cells across different age groups, filtering for candidates that could roll back "transcriptomic age" (how old a cell looks based on its gene expression patterns). Splicing factors are regulatory proteins that simultaneously influence how thousands of genes are read — tweaking one is like flipping multiple aging switches at once. In cell experiments, SRSF1 overexpression reduced cellular senescence, protein aggregation, and chronic inflammation, all without stripping cells of their original identity — which is exactly the core problem that Yamanaka factors (another rejuvenation approach, but one that carries tumorigenic risk) have long struggled with. Delivered into aged mice, SRSF1 extended lifespan by 14%. Results so far are mouse-only, and whether this holds up in humans is still an open question. Check out [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/splicing-factor-srsf1-overexpression-produces-a-more-youthful-transcriptome-and-slows-aging-in-mice/) for details on the screening method and cell phenotype data.

### 2. Pig Kidney Stored at -150°C for One Year Successfully Produces Urine After Transplant

This one's a genuine milestone for organ banking. 🫘 Researchers perfused pig kidney vasculature with iron oxide nanoparticles, then vitrified the organ (a deep-freeze method that avoids ice crystal damage) down to -150°C for up to one year. On retrieval, an alternating electromagnetic field caused the nanoparticles to generate heat uniformly from within, solving the classic problem of uneven rewarming between the surface and deep layers of large organs. The rewarmed kidneys maintained vascular, metabolic, and excretory function during ex vivo perfusion, and produced urine immediately after transplantation — nearly matching traditional cold-storage controls. This technique had previously only been validated on small rodent kidneys; this is the first success at human-organ scale, opening a real path toward long-term organ banking. The catch: nanoparticles must be injected before freezing, so this can't be applied to already-preserved tissue. Head to [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/pig-kidneys-remain-functional-after-vitrification-with-iron-oxide-nanoparticles-rewarming-and-transplantion/) for rewarming protocol details.

### 3. Lysosomal Metabolite Accumulation Predicts Cell Age — Caloric Restriction Helps Everywhere Except the Brain

Lysosomes are the cell's "recycling center," and it turns out they keep a clock. ⏱️ Researchers isolated lysosomes from multiple species and tissue types, running metabolomics and lipidomics analyses. They found three metabolites that consistently accumulate in lysosomes with age: glycerophosphodiesters (GPD), cystine, and a membrane lipid called BMP. This accumulation pattern is conserved across species and can be used to predict a cell's chronological age — a "lysosomal aging clock." Caloric restriction slows these changes in peripheral tissues, but has no effect in the brain. The kicker? The cells showing the most dramatic accumulation are neurons and microglia — the very cells at the heart of neurodegenerative disease. Whether these metabolites are just passive markers of aging or active drivers is still unknown. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/a-lysosomal-aging-clock-and-novel-signature-of-lysosomal-aging/) for the analytical methods and cross-species data.

### 4. A Natural Anti-Amyloid Peptide Found in Blood — Alzheimer's Patients Have Less of It

The peptide HAEE is making an unexpected case for itself as both a biomarker and a therapeutic candidate. 🧠 Researchers originally designed it as a synthetic peptide to block amyloid-β (Aβ, the protein that abnormally aggregates in Alzheimer's patients' brains) from binding zinc ions, thereby preventing Aβ aggregation. While setting up a blood detection method ahead of clinical trials, they stumbled on something surprising: HAEE is already a natural component of human plasma. When they compared samples from 200 Alzheimer's patients and 150 cognitively normal controls, patients had significantly lower endogenous HAEE levels. This positions HAEE deficiency as a potential diagnostic marker and synthetic supplementation as a candidate therapy — though the work is still observational, and causality plus clinical efficacy need trial validation. Check out [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/engineered-peptide-that-suppresses-amyloid-%ce%b2-aggregation-found-to-exist-in-the-body/) for detection methods and sample details.

### 5. The Gut-Bone Connection: Aging Gut Microbiome Drives Bone Loss Through the Immune System

Osteoporosis has a gut problem. 🦠 A new review lays out how age-related gut microbiome dysbiosis accelerates bone loss via the bone marrow immune microenvironment. Bone is constantly remodeled — osteoblasts build it up, osteoclasts break it down — and when that balance tips, osteoporosis follows. An aging microbiome worsens gut barrier leakiness, letting inflammatory signals flood into the bloodstream and reach bone marrow, where they activate more osteoclasts. On top of that, it may influence bone marrow immune cell behavior through the vagus and sympathetic nerves. This framework stretches the cause of osteoporosis from "local bone degeneration" to a whole "gut-nerve-immune-bone" system — though the relative importance of each pathway is still contested. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/reviewing-what-is-known-of-how-the-aging-gut-microbiome-may-contribute-to-osteoporosis/) for the specific mechanisms covered in the review.

### 6. Cold Stress and Longevity: Muscle-Derived BH2 Crosses Tissues to Protect Proteins

Mild cold extends lifespan in multiple species, but the mechanism has been murky — until now. 🥶 Researchers working in nematodes found that low temperature boosts synthesis of a metabolite called BH2 in muscle tissue. BH2 travels from muscle to the gut and neurons, where it's converted into the active cofactor BH4, which helps cells synthesize tyrosine and suppress protein aggregation (a hallmark shared by Huntington's disease, ALS, and other neurodegenerative conditions). Just boosting BH2 synthesis in muscle alone extended nematode lifespan at normal temperatures and reduced protein aggregation in distal tissues. In human cell experiments, supplementing with BH2 or tyrosine also reduced aggregation linked to Huntington's and ALS. Most results are still in worms and cells, so human validation is needed. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/investigating-the-mechanisms-of-cold-hormesis/) for the cross-species experimental design.

### 7. Cancer Can Be Driven by Epigenetic Changes Alone — No DNA Mutations Required

The standard cancer story is built on DNA mutations, but this mouse study pokes a significant hole in that narrative. 🔬 Researchers activated the transcriptional co-activator YAP1 in a mouse liver cancer model. Whole-exome sequencing found no typical driver gene mutations in the liver cells — yet DNA demethylation (an epigenetic change, meaning the "on/off switch" state of genes changed without altering the actual sequence) switched on multiple oncogenes. Knocking down Tet1, a gene that promotes demethylation, significantly reduced liver tumor formation. The researchers also found the same molecular signature in liver samples from patients with a congenital heart condition associated with liver cancer. The upshot: the epigenetic chaos that accumulates during aging may itself be a cancer driver, not just a bystander to DNA mutations. Mechanisms are primarily from mouse work for now. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/cancer-can-emerge-from-epigenetic-change-rather-than-dna-mutation/) for single-cell spatial transcriptomics results.

### 8. MicroRNA miR-3155b Found to Suppress Inflammatory Signaling in Senescent Cells

Meet a potential new tool for quieting the SASP fire without needing to kill the cells. 🔕 Senescent cells don't die — they just sit there endlessly pumping out pro-inflammatory signals, a phenomenon called SASP (Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype), which is a key driver of tissue aging and cancer treatment side effects. Working in a chemotherapy-induced senescence model using breast cancer cells, researchers found that a small RNA called miR-3155b is significantly downregulated during senescence. Artificially restoring its levels suppressed expression of multiple pro-inflammatory factors — IL-6, IL-8, TNF — without changing the senescent state itself (cells still stopped dividing, DNA damage markers remained). So it dials down the "fire" without disbanding the "old guard." This is a senomorphic strategy (modifying senescent cell behavior) rather than a senolytic one (clearing them out). Results are cell-only so far. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/mir-3155b-is-a-senomorphic-microrna-reducing-senescent-cell-inflammatory-signaling/) for target validation details.

### 9. A Hidden Risk in Stem Cell Therapy: "Toxic Exosomes" Generated During Culture

Stem cell therapies may have a quality control problem baked into the manufacturing process. ⚠️ When stem cells are expanded in vitro, some inevitably enter senescence — and those senescent cells release exosomes (nanoscale vesicles cells use to communicate) loaded with misfolded proteins, particularly oxidatively damaged SOD1. These "toxic exosomes" trigger cascading senescence in recipient cells, a phenomenon already documented in neural stem cell lines. The paper calls for senescent cell percentage and exosome proteome integrity to be added as standard quality metrics in stem cell production — though none of this is currently a regulatory requirement, and similar risks in mesenchymal stem cell and iPSC-derived products still need independent validation. Check out [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/aspects-of-stem-cell-biology-that-impair-the-quality-of-stem-cell-therapies/) for the specific proposed quality control steps.

### 10. Alzheimer's May Be a Whole-Body Amyloid Imbalance — and the Liver Is the Key Clearance Organ

Alzheimer's disease, long treated as a brain-local problem, may actually be a systemic one. 🫀 A new review pulls together emerging evidence that brain amyloid-β (Aβ) and blood Aβ exist in dynamic equilibrium — clearing Aβ from the blood can pull Aβ out of the brain. The review maps four brain drainage pathways (including the blood-brain barrier and the glymphatic system) plus peripheral clearance by the liver, kidneys, and gut. The liver emerges as the primary organ for peripheral Aβ elimination, and the root cause of brain Aβ deposition isn't any single pathway failing — it's the coordinated collapse of multiple pathways together. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/holistically-considering-amyloid-%ce%b2-in-the-whole-body-in-the-context-of-alzheimers-disease/) for details on each clearance pathway and combination therapy ideas. This framework is still in the theoretical and early-trial stage.

## **⌘ Top Open Source Projects**

### agnikitha/retfound-cvd-screening: Estimating Cardiovascular Risk and Retinal Biological Age from Fundus Images

This project is built on top of RETFound (a vision foundation model pretrained specifically on retinal images), and it's a pretty ambitious platform. 👁️ It handles cardiovascular risk-related metrics (carotid intima-media thickness, or CIMT, a marker of arterial stiffness), retinal biological age estimation, and five-disease classification — all in one. Retinal vasculature is essentially a natural window into systemic vascular health, and RETFound captures health-relevant image features by pretraining on large-scale fundus datasets. The project description frames this as a non-invasive screening tool, but a heads-up: correlation between image features and these metrics doesn't automatically mean it can accurately measure specific clinical values. Actual precision and applicable populations need to be verified against the evaluation results in the repo. Repo: [agnikitha/retfound-cvd-screening](https://github.com/agnikitha/retfound-cvd-screening), last active late October 2026.

## **🧪 Cutting-Edge Research**

### "Immature" Astrocytes in the Brain Correlate with Cognitive Decline

Astrocytes are stepping out of the support role. 🧩 Researchers analyzed hippocampal tissue surgically removed from multiple adult patients during epilepsy surgery and found that a subtype of "immature astrocytes" marked by Dcx+ increases with age — and their numbers correlate with the degree of decline in fluid intelligence (a cognitive measure of how quickly someone can learn and solve new problems). Astrocytes have traditionally been seen as support cells for neurons, but growing evidence shows they actively participate in learning and memory regulation. The key limitation here: the samples came from epilepsy patients, whose brains tend to age faster than the general population, so whether this correlation holds more broadly is still uncertain, and the mechanism needs follow-up work. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/age-related-decline-in-fluid-intelligence-correlates-with-the-presence-of-immature-astroglia/) for the cognitive assessment methods and cell count data.
