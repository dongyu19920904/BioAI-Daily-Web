## **🔥 Today's Top 10 Highlights**

### 1. Splicing Factor SRSF1 Extends Lifespan in Aged Mice by 14%

SRSF1, a splicing factor, was pinpointed through a machine-learning scan of human skin cell gene expression profiles across different age groups — the goal was to find genes that could roll back "transcriptomic age" (the degree of cellular aging reflected by gene expression patterns). Splicing factors are regulatory proteins that can simultaneously influence how thousands of genes are read, so tweaking one is like flipping multiple aging switches at once. In cell experiments, SRSF1 overexpression reduced cellular senescence, protein aggregation, and chronic inflammation — and crucially, cells didn't lose their original identity. That last point matters because it's the exact problem that has long plagued Yamanaka factors (another rejuvenation approach, but one that carries tumor risk). When SRSF1 was delivered into aged mice, lifespan extended by 14%. 🐭 Results are mice-only so far, and whether this translates to humans is still an open question. Check out [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/splicing-factor-srsf1-overexpression-produces-a-more-youthful-transcriptome-and-slows-aging-in-mice/) for details on the screening method and cell phenotype findings.

### 2. Pig Kidney Stored at -150°C for a Year Successfully Transplanted and Produced Urine 🧊

Researchers perfused pig kidney blood vessels with iron oxide nanoparticles, then vitrified the organs (a deep-cryopreservation method that avoids ice crystal damage) down to -150°C for up to a year. On retrieval, alternating electromagnetic fields caused the nanoparticles to generate heat uniformly from within, solving the classic problem of uneven rewarming between the surface and deep layers of large organs. The rewarmed kidneys maintained vascular, metabolic, and secretory function during ex vivo perfusion, and immediately produced urine after transplantation — with functional recovery close to the traditional cold-storage control group. This technique had previously only been validated on small rodent kidneys, so pulling it off at human-organ scale is a genuine milestone and opens a real path toward long-term organ banking. One catch: the nanoparticles must be injected before freezing, so this can't be applied to already-stored tissue. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/pig-kidneys-remain-functional-after-vitrification-with-iron-oxide-nanoparticles-rewarming-and-transplantion/) for rewarming protocol details.

### 3. Lysosomal Metabolite Accumulation Predicts Cell Age — Caloric Restriction Helps, But Not in the Brain 🔬

Lysosomes, the cell's "recycling centers," are responsible for breaking down worn-out proteins and structures. Researchers isolated lysosomes from multiple species and tissue types, then ran metabolomic and lipidomic analyses. They found three metabolites that stably accumulate in lysosomes with age: glycerophosphodiesters (GPD), cystine, and the membrane lipid BMP. This accumulation pattern is conserved across species and can be used to predict a cell's chronological age — effectively a "lysosomal clock." Caloric restriction (eating less) slows these changes in peripheral tissues, but has no effect in the brain. And here's the kicker: the cells where accumulation is most pronounced are neurons and microglia — the very cells central to neurodegenerative disease. Whether these metabolites are merely markers of aging or active drivers of it remains unclear. Check out [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/a-lysosomal-aging-clock-and-novel-signature-of-lysosomal-aging/) for analysis methods and cross-species data.

### 4. A Natural Anti-Amyloid Peptide Found in Blood — Lower in Alzheimer's Patients 🧠

HAEE, a synthetic peptide originally designed to block amyloid-β (Aβ) from binding zinc ions and thus prevent its aggregation in Alzheimer's patients' brains, took a surprising turn during prep for clinical trials. While developing a blood detection method, researchers discovered that HAEE is actually a natural component of human plasma. A follow-up comparison of 200 Alzheimer's patients versus 150 cognitively normal controls found significantly lower endogenous HAEE concentrations in the patient group. This suggests HAEE deficiency could be a diagnostic biomarker, and synthetic HAEE supplementation becomes a candidate therapeutic direction — but we're still in observational territory, and causality plus clinical efficacy need proper trial validation. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/engineered-peptide-that-suppresses-amyloid-%ce%b2-aggregation-found-to-exist-in-the-body/) for detection methods and sample details.

### 5. Aging Gut Microbiome Contributes to Osteoporosis via the Immune System 💀

A new review maps out how age-related gut dysbiosis drives bone loss through the bone marrow immune microenvironment. Bone is constantly being built by osteoblasts and broken down by osteoclasts — when that balance tips, osteoporosis follows. An aging microbiome worsens gut barrier leakage, letting inflammatory signals spill into the bloodstream and reach the bone marrow, activating more osteoclasts. On top of that, it may also influence bone marrow immune cell behavior through the vagus and sympathetic nervous systems. This framework expands the etiology of osteoporosis from "local bone degeneration" to a full "gut-neural-immune-bone" systems view — though the relative importance of each pathway is still debated. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/reviewing-what-is-known-of-how-the-aging-gut-microbiome-may-contribute-to-osteoporosis/) for the specific mechanisms covered.

### 6. Cold Stress Longevity Mechanism: Muscle-Derived BH2 Delivers Cross-Tissue Protein Protection ❄️

Mild cold exposure extends lifespan across multiple species, but the mechanism has been murky. Researchers working in C. elegans found that low temperatures boost synthesis of a metabolite called BH2 in muscle tissue. BH2 travels from muscle to the gut and neurons, where it's converted into the active cofactor BH4, which then helps cells synthesize tyrosine and suppress protein aggregation — a hallmark of Huntington's disease, ALS, and other neurodegenerative conditions. Boosting BH2 synthesis in muscle alone was enough to extend worm lifespan at normal temperature and reduce protein aggregation in distal tissues. In human cell experiments, supplementing BH2 or tyrosine also reduced protein aggregation linked to Huntington's and ALS. Most results are still from worms and cell experiments, and human effects need independent validation. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/investigating-the-mechanisms-of-cold-hormesis/) for cross-species experimental design details.

### 7. Cancer Can Be Driven by Epigenetic Changes — No DNA Mutations Required 🧬

The conventional story says cancer requires DNA mutations, but researchers working in a mouse liver cancer model have challenged that. After activating the transcriptional co-activator YAP1, liver cells showed no typical driver gene mutations on whole-exome sequencing — yet they activated multiple oncogenes through DNA demethylation (an epigenetic change, meaning the gene's "on/off switch" state changed, not the sequence itself). Knocking down the pro-demethylation gene Tet1 significantly reduced liver tumor formation. Researchers also found the same molecular signature in liver samples from patients with a congenital heart defect associated with liver cancer. The takeaway: epigenetic dysregulation accumulated during aging may itself be a driver of cancer, not just a bystander to DNA mutations. Mechanistic results are primarily from mouse experiments. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/cancer-can-emerge-from-epigenetic-change-rather-than-dna-mutation/) for single-cell spatial transcriptomics results.

### 8. microRNA miR-3155b Found to Suppress Inflammatory Signaling in Senescent Cells 🔥

Senescent cells don't die — they just keep pumping out pro-inflammatory signals to their surroundings, a phenomenon called SASP (senescence-associated secretory phenotype). It's a major driver of tissue aging and post-cancer-treatment side effects. In a chemotherapy-induced senescence model using breast cancer cells, researchers found that miR-3155b, a small RNA, was significantly downregulated during senescence. Artificially raising its levels suppressed expression of multiple pro-inflammatory factors including IL-6, IL-8, and TNF — but didn't change the senescent state itself (cells still stopped dividing and still showed DNA damage markers). So it damps the flames without clearing out the old cells. That makes miR-3155b a senomorphic strategy rather than a senolytic one. Results are cell-experiment-only for now. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/mir-3155b-is-a-senomorphic-microrna-reducing-senescent-cell-inflammatory-signaling/) for target validation details.

### 9. A Hidden Risk in Stem Cell Therapy: "Toxic Exosomes" Generated During Cell Culture ⚠️

When stem cells are expanded in vitro, some inevitably enter a senescent state and start releasing exosomes (nanoscale vesicles cells use to communicate) loaded with misfolded proteins — particularly oxidatively damaged SOD1. These "toxic exosomes" can trigger cascading senescence in recipient cells, a phenomenon already documented in neural stem cell line experiments. The article calls for adding senescent cell percentage and exosome proteome integrity as routine quality control metrics in stem cell manufacturing — but these aren't currently regulatory requirements. Similar risks in mesenchymal stem cell and iPSC-derived products also await independent validation. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/aspects-of-stem-cell-biology-that-impair-the-quality-of-stem-cell-therapies/) for specific quality control proposals.

### 10. Alzheimer's Disease May Be a Systemic Amyloid Imbalance — and the Liver Is Key 🫀

Alzheimer's has long been treated as a brain-local problem, but a new review pulls together emerging evidence for a different picture: brain amyloid-β (Aβ) and blood Aβ exist in dynamic equilibrium, and clearing Aβ from the bloodstream can drive brain Aβ to migrate outward. The review systematically maps four brain drainage pathways (including the blood-brain barrier and glymphatic system) as well as peripheral clearance via the liver, kidneys, and gut. The liver emerges as the primary organ for peripheral Aβ elimination — and the root cause of Aβ accumulation in the brain isn't a single pathway failing, but the coordinated collapse of multiple pathways together. The framework is still in the theoretical and early-trial stage. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/holistically-considering-amyloid-%ce%b2-in-the-whole-body-in-the-context-of-alzheimers-disease/) for details on individual clearance pathways and combination therapy ideas.

## **⌘ Open Source Top Projects**

### agnikitha/retfound-cvd-screening: Estimating Cardiovascular Risk and Retinal Biological Age from Fundus Images 👁️

Built on top of RETFound (a large vision model pretrained specifically on retinal images), this project is an AI platform that simultaneously handles cardiovascular risk-related metrics (carotid intima-media thickness CIMT, a marker for arterial stiffness), retinal biological age estimation, and five-disease classification. Retinal blood vessels are a natural "window" into systemic vascular health, and RETFound's large-scale fundus image pretraining captures image features correlated with whole-body health status. The project description lists non-invasive screening as the use case — but correlation between image features and these metrics doesn't equal accurate measurement of specific test values. Actual precision and applicable populations need to be checked against the evaluation results in the repo. Repo: [agnikitha/retfound-cvd-screening](https://github.com/agnikitha/retfound-cvd-screening), last active late October 2026.

## **🧪 Frontier Research**

### "Immature" Astroglia in the Brain Correlates with Cognitive Decline 🧩

Using hippocampal tissue resected during epilepsy surgery (from multiple adult patients), researchers found that Dcx+ "immature astroglia" increase with age — and their count correlates with the degree of decline in fluid intelligence (the cognitive metric measuring how quickly someone learns and solves novel problems). Astrocytes are typically seen as support cells for neurons, but mounting evidence shows they actively participate in regulating learning and memory. The main limitation here: the samples came from epilepsy patients, whose brains age faster than average, so whether this correlation applies to the general population is uncertain, and the mechanism needs follow-up. See [the Fight Aging! report](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/age-related-decline-in-fluid-intelligence-correlates-with-the-presence-of-immature-astroglia/) for cognitive assessment methods and cell count data.
