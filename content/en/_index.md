## 🔥 Top 10 Stories

### 1. [AI-Designed Lung Fibrosis Drug Reverses Biological Age by Three Years](https://lifespan.io/lung-fibrosis-drug-lowers-biological-age-estimates/)
People used to think drugs just treat disease—now AI-designed drugs might make you a few years younger on the side. Insilico's rentosertib, developed using their AI platform, not only improved lung function in pulmonary fibrosis patients during clinical trials, but six different protein clocks detected that patients' blood protein profiles became younger—patients on the 60mg dose showed an average biological age decrease of about three years after four weeks. This doesn't mean patients literally "turned back time," but their blood protein composition became more like younger people. Interestingly, these changes didn't completely sync with lung function improvements, hinting the drug might genuinely be affecting aging processes more broadly. Of course, fibrosis, metabolism, and aging are already intertwined, so whether this drug specifically treats lung disease or incidentally fights aging needs more investigation.

Want to know if a supplement or therapy actually works? Forever Healthy Foundation adapted SpaceX's Grok AI into Evipedia Grok Bot, specifically serving the longevity community. ### 3. [Starting Growth Hormone Suppression in Midlife Still Extends Mouse Lifespan Nearly 10%](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/09/midlife-growth-hormone-receptor-ablation-modestly-slows-aging-in-mice/)
Suppressing growth hormone metabolism extends mouse lifespan—this has been studied for decades. Previous experiments all started interventions from birth, but now someone tried starting during "midlife crisis"—12-month-old mice (equivalent to human midlife) before knocking out the growth hormone receptor gene. Result: male mouse lifespan still extended, though not as dramatically as lifelong intervention, but still nearly 10%. Female mice showed weaker effects. Interestingly, while these mice had better insulin sensitivity, preserved neuromuscular function and bone microstructure, they gained more fat. Human Laron syndrome patients (born with deficient growth hormone receptor function) have lower incidence of certain diseases but no clear lifespan extension, so this approach might have limited utility for humans.

### 4. [The Secret of Immortal Hydra: Never-Ending Telomerase Expression](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/09/telomerase-expression-in-the-immortal-hydra/)
Most animals age, but a few hydra species are exceptions—they don't age in normal environments and mortality doesn't rise with time. This time researchers focused on telomerase in hydra. Telomerase is a "life-extension enzyme" that can lengthen telomeres at chromosome ends, preventing cells from dying due to replication. Human somatic cells barely express telomerase (so we age), only stem cells, germ cells and cancer cells express it abundantly. Hydra are different—they're basically walking masses of stem cells, any body part can regenerate. Research found that hydra telomerase catalytic subunit (hyTERT) continuously expresses in high-proliferation regions, staying stable during both budding reproduction and regeneration. Hydra "immortality" probably relies on this trick. Of course, whether humans can learn anything remains unclear—our body structure is far more complex.

### 5. [Lilly's GIP/GLP-1 Dual Agonist Shows Early Promise in Mental and Addiction Disorders](https://www.genengnews.com/topics/translational-medicine/lillys-gip-glp-1-candidate-brenipatide-shows-early-clinical-promise-in-substance-psych-disorders/)
Weight-loss wonder drug tirzepatide made Lilly a fortune, now they're taking brenipatide with the same mechanism and targeting mental illness and addiction disorders. This time it's not weight loss, it's "quitting alcohol" and "antidepressant." Phase I trials showed brenipatide has a long half-life of 9-12 days, supporting once-weekly dosing, with better gastrointestinal tolerability than existing GLP-1 drugs. Lilly skipped Phase II and launched two Phase III trials: one for major depressive disorder (MDD), another for alcohol use disorder (AUD). Depression affects 322 million globally, alcoholism 400 million—these two indications represent huge markets. More critically, existing AUD drugs assume patients aim for "complete abstinence," but brenipatide focuses on "changing drinking patterns"—for people who can't completely quit, this might be more realistic. Lilly is also testing its effects on bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, tobacco and opioid addiction.

### 6. [One Immune Switch May Drive Whole-Body Aging](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260901010717.htm)
Aging might partly result from immune cells "slacking off"—they stop clearing damaged, inflammation-producing cells. Researchers blocked a receptor called PD-L2 in mice, restoring immune clearance function, reducing inflammation, and keeping organs, muscles and memory remarkably young. PD-L2 is part of immune checkpoints, meant to prevent the immune system from attacking normal cells. But senescent cells also use it as a "shield" to evade immune pursuit. Once this switch is turned off, senescent cells can't hide. This study suggests targeting PD-L2 might be a new strategy for clearing senescent cells—adding another path to existing senolytics. However, immune checkpoints themselves are important, systemic inhibition could trigger autoimmune issues, so the future challenge is how to precisely target senescent cells.

### 7. [Impaired Lactate Metabolism in Liver and Muscle May Be Primary Cause of Sarcopenia](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/09/impaired-lactate-metabolism-in-liver-and-muscle-as-an-important-cause-of-sarcopenia/)
Sarcopenia—severe loss of muscle mass and strength in elderly—has always had unclear causes. Some say muscle stem cells fail, others blame inflammation or hormonal changes. Now there's a new theory: maybe it's lactate metabolism problems. Research found aged mice have declining liver lactate processing capacity, causing lactate accumulation in skeletal muscle, leading to intracellular acidification (lactic acidosis), which damages muscle function. More interestingly, activating hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) or specifically activating HIF1α in the liver can improve lactate tolerance in aged mice, reduce muscle acidosis, and alleviate sarcopenia. This discovery pulls the liver into sarcopenia's causal chain—aging isn't a single organ issue but multi-organ coordination failure. Future treatments might not just target muscles but also restore liver metabolic capacity.

### 8. [Telomeres and Lifespan: Caloric Restriction Slows DNA Damage Accumulation](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/09/calorie-restriction-slows-the-accumulation-of-nuclear-dna-damage/)
Caloric restriction (CR) extending lifespan has been tested in animals for decades, but how exactly does it work? Researchers found CR slows DNA mutation accumulation rates in multiple mouse tissues—including substitution mutations (one DNA letter swapped for another) and insertion/deletion mutations (adding or missing DNA letters). Interestingly, liver cell mutation reduction was most obvious, and CR's protective effect was strongest in "inactive regions" of the genome (no genes or unused genes). Why? Probably because active regions already frequently repair DNA damage, so CR brings little extra benefit. This study first confirmed CR's impact on mutation burden across the whole genome. However, CR simultaneously improves cell function, metabolism, inflammation and many other things, so it's unclear how much DNA damage reduction contributes to lifespan extension.

### 9. [Transformation of Vascular Smooth Muscle into Macrophages in Atherosclerotic Plaques](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/09/the-transformation-of-vascular-smooth-muscle-into-macrophages-in-atherosclerotic-plaques/)
Atherosclerosis is the number one killer—plaque rupture causes heart attacks or strokes. Plaques are essentially macrophage "graveyards"—macrophages are attracted to plaques trying to repair them, but get killed by the toxic environment and become part of the plaque themselves. Even more absurd, vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMC) around blood vessels aren't quiet either—they transform into macrophage-like cells, further accelerating plaque growth—almost like cancer self-proliferation. Researchers used single-cell omics and lineage tracing to reveal VSMC heterogeneity and clonal expansion, finding they transition from contractile state to synthetic, macrophage-like, foam cell-like and fibroblast-like states. Targeting VSMC plasticity might be a new direction for treating atherosclerosis—like phenotype-specific regulation, immunotherapy, nanomedicine or senotherapeutics.

### 10. [Glial Reprogramming into Neurons Becomes Much Less Efficient with Advancing Age](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/09/reprogramming-glia-into-neurons-becomes-less-effective-with-advancing-age/)
Reprogramming brain glial cells into neurons can replace neurons lost to neurodegenerative diseases—this is a hot direction in regenerative medicine. But most research uses young mice—what about aged mice? Efficiency drops significantly. Researchers experimented with retinas (because retinas are part of the central nervous system but easier to study), finding aged Müller glial cells have dramatically reduced neurogenic capacity. Single-cell transcriptomics showed aged glial cells cannot activate progenitor cell programs, instead entering reactive and inflammatory states. Meanwhile, aged retinas show stronger neuroimmune responses to injury. Immune modulation with dexamethasone partially restored neurogenesis. This suggests aging sets both "intrinsic barriers" and "microenvironment barriers" for glial reprogramming—future treatments might need to address both inflammation and glial cell state simultaneously.

## 📎 Citable Points Today

### 1. AI-designed drug rentosertib reduces biological age markers
- **Factual conclusion**: Insilico's AI-designed drug rentosertib reduced predicted biological age detected by 6 protein clocks in a 12-week clinical trial for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis patients, with the 60mg single-dose group averaging about 3 years younger at week 4.
- **Original source**: [Lung Fibrosis Drug Lowers Biological Age Estimates](https://lifespan.io/lung-fibrosis-drug-lowers-biological-age-estimates/)
- **Evidence boundaries**: This was a 12-week, 71-participant (42 analyzed) Phase 2a randomized controlled trial. Biological age reduction is based on blood protein profile changes, not actual lifespan or all-cause mortality data. Since fibrosis, metabolism and aging mechanisms are highly intertwined, it cannot yet be determined whether the drug has broad anti-aging effects beyond fibrosis treatment. Lung function improvement and biological age changes were not completely correlated, suggesting possible broader biological effects, but longer-term, larger-scale studies are needed for verification.

### 2. Blocking PD-L2 restores immune system's ability to clear senescent cells
- **Factual conclusion**: Research found senescent cells express PD-L2 protein to evade immune clearance; knocking out or blocking PD-L2 in mice reduced senescent cell accumulation and improved insulin sensitivity and grip strength.
- **Original source**: [One immune switch may help drive aging across the body](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260901010717.htm)
- **Evidence boundaries**: This is mouse model research, not yet validated in humans. PD-L2 is an immune checkpoint protein; systemic inhibition may trigger autoimmune risks, similar to checkpoint inhibitor side effects in cancer immunotherapy. The study did not clarify long-term impacts of PD-L2 blockade on normal tissue function or determine optimal targeting strategies (systemic vs local). Whether human senescent cells use PD-L2 the same way and the therapeutic window of this strategy still require clinical research.

### 3. Declining liver lactate metabolism capacity may be root cause of sarcopenia
- **Factual conclusion**: Research found aged mouse livers have declining lactate processing capacity, causing skeletal muscle lactate accumulation and intracellular acidification; pharmacological or liver-specific activation of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF1α) improved lactate tolerance and alleviated sarcopenia.
- **Original source**: [Impaired Lactate Metabolism in Liver and Muscle as an Important Cause of Sarcopenia](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/09/impaired-lactate-metabolism-in-liver-and-muscle-as-an-important-cause-of-sarcopenia/)
- **Evidence boundaries**: This is mouse model research focused on liver-muscle lactate metabolism axis mechanisms. Human sarcopenia etiology is complex, involving muscle stem cell function, inflammation, hormonal changes and other factors; the relative importance of lactate metabolism dysfunction remains unclear. Systemic effects of HIF1α activation (like impacts on cardiovascular system, cancer risk) were not fully assessed in the study. Human studies are needed to confirm causal relationship between liver lactate processing capacity and sarcopenia, plus safety and efficacy of HIF activation.
