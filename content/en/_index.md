## **🔥 Today's Top 8 Highlights**

### 1. SRSF1 Overexpression Cuts Mouse Frailty by 44%, Extends Median Lifespan by 14%

A Harvard Medical School team published research in *Nature Aging* identifying SRSF1, an RNA splicing factor—a regulatory protein that "splices" gene information as cells read it—as a potential rejuvenation target. Researchers first trained a machine learning aging clock based on gene expression from human skin cells aged 1 to 96 years, using it to screen genes linked to human aging networks. They found that SRSF1 overexpression rivaled the effect of the famous OSKM reprogramming cocktail without causing cells to lose their identity. In aged mice, viral vector-induced SRSF1 expression reduced frailty by 44% and extended median lifespan by 14% in cohorts of 9-10 female mice. The viral vector currently targets only the liver, limiting systemic effects, and precise dose and timing control remain to be studied; translating from mice to human therapy still requires a long development path. For further details on trial design and molecular mechanisms, see [Lifespan.io's coverage](https://lifespan.io/rejuvenation-factor-extends-lifespan-and-healthspan-in-mice/).

### 2. MTX325 Targets Mitochondrial Clearance in First Parkinson's Patient Dosing

Mission Therapeutics announced completion of first-patient dosing for its Phase 1b Parkinson's trial at Liverpool Royal University Hospital in the UK. MTX325 is an oral, blood-brain-barrier-penetrant USP30 inhibitor; USP30 acts as a "brake" preventing cells from clearing damaged mitochondria—the cell's "power plants"—and inhibiting it promotes removal of impaired mitochondria. The trial plans to enroll 36 mild-to-moderate patients across the UK, Europe, and Australia, aiming to verify whether the drug produces biological changes in humans consistent with animal experiments, not directly assessing symptom improvement; data readout is expected by end of 2027. Early animal studies and Phase 1a trials in healthy volunteers suggested brain penetration, but a long path remains from safety signals to Parkinson's symptom improvement. Details at [Longevity.Technology's report](https://longevity.technology/news/first-parkinsons-patient-dosed-with-usp30-inhibitor/).

### 3. Percival AI Model Identifies 351 Disease-Associated Signals From Routine CT Scans

A University of Pennsylvania team published research in *npj Digital Medicine* developing Percival, a vision-language model—an AI that simultaneously understands images and text—trained on paired data from 52,000 participants with over 400,000 CT scans and radiology reports. The model found statistical associations between scan image features and 351 diagnostic categories, and outperformed models using only age, sex, or organ measurements in predicting future onset of heart failure, cirrhosis, and acute kidney injury. However, the study notes that CT image features being associated with these diseases does not equal accurately measuring blood markers; some "predicted" future diagnoses may have been present but unrecorded at scan time. The study also did not directly compare against radiologists. More evaluation details at [Lifespan.io's coverage](https://lifespan.io/ai-extracts-broader-health-insights-from-routine-ct-scans/).

### 4. SynBITE Programmable Cell Deletion Circuit Preserves Organoid Vasculature

A University of Pittsburgh team published research in *Cell Stem Cell* constructing SynBITE, a genetic circuit that clears unwanted immature cells in situ during organoid development—lab-grown miniature organ mimics—without first dissociating and reassembling tissue. This addresses a silent cost: traditional purification methods destroy formed vascular networks, whereas in situ deletion preserved approximately 23% more vessel length and 122% more vascular nodes. Combined with CRISPR activation, treated liver organoids achieved drug metabolism activity around 60% of primary hepatocytes, versus about 20% in non-apoptosis-induced controls. Researchers noted similar subtractive logic might eventually clear senescent cells in living tissue, but this remains conceptual; the circuit's core components are borrowed from CAR-T therapy safety switches but play a more central role here.
